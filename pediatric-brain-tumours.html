Pediatric Brain Tumors

What Are Pediatric Brain Tumors?

Think of the brain as a busy city where different neighborhoods handle different jobs—like moving, seeing, talking and thinking. Millions of tiny building blocks called cells live in these neighborhoods, growing and replacing themselves in an orderly, controlled way.

A pediatric brain tumor happens when a group of these cells starts multiplying faster than normal, forming a cluster or lump inside the brain or skull.

Because the skull is a firm, fixed bone container with limited room inside, even a non-cancerous (benign) mass can cause symptoms simply by taking up space, pressing against sensitive brain tissue or blocking the brain's natural fluid pathways.

Common Types in Children

  • Astrocytomas & Gliomas: Tumors that grow from star-shaped support cells in the brain. They range from slow-growing (like Pilocytic Astrocytoma) to more active forms.
  • Medulloblastoma: A fast-growing tumor located in the back of the brain (the cerebellum) that affects balance and coordination.
  • Ependymoma: A tumor that forms near the fluid-filled spaces (ventricles) inside the brain or spinal cord.
  • Craniopharyngioma: A benign tumor near the base of the brain close to the pituitary gland, which can affect vision and growth hormones.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because a child's brain is constantly growing, symptoms depend heavily on the tumor's location and whether it is creating pressure inside the head:

Morning Headaches & Vomiting

Headaches that are worse when waking up in the morning, often accompanied by sudden, unexplained vomiting (which briefly relieves the headache).

Balance & Movement Changes

Unsteadiness while walking, frequent tripping, clumsiness or weakness on one side of the body.

Vision Changes

Double vision, blurry sight or strange eye movements (like rapid twitching or eyes turning inward).

Unusual Fatigue or Mood Changes

Marked changes in energy levels, increased sleepiness or subtle shifts in behavior and school performance.

Seizures

Unexpected twitching, staring spells or convulsions in a child with no prior history.

Head Circumference Changes (in Babies)

In infants whose skull bones haven't fused yet, a rapid increase in head size or a bulging soft spot (fontanelle).

How Is It Discovered?

If a brain tumor is suspected, specialized imaging provides clear answers quickly without pain:

  • Neurological Examination: A comprehensive check of your child's reflexes, eye movements, balance, hearing and coordination.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The primary diagnostic tool. An MRI uses safe magnetic fields to build detailed 3D pictures of the brain, showing the exact size, location and characteristics of the tumor.
  • Computed Tomography (CT) Scan: Often used in emergency situations to quickly check for pressure buildup or fluid blockage.
  • Biopsy or Tissue Analysis: A tiny sample of the tumor is examined under a microscope by a pathologist to identify its exact cell type, helping tailor the safest treatment plan.

How Is It Treated?

Treating pediatric brain tumors involves a compassionate team of specialists (pediatric neurosurgeons, oncologists and radiation experts). The approach depends on the tumor's type and location:

  • Neurosurgical Removal: When safe, a neurosurgeon performs surgery to remove as much of the tumor as possible while preserving surrounding healthy brain tissue and protecting essential functions.
  • Relieving Brain Pressure: If the tumor blocks fluid drainage, surgeons can place a temporary drain, a VP shunt or perform a quick endoscopic procedure (ETV) to restore normal fluid flow.
  • Targeted Therapies (Chemotherapy / Radiation): Depending on the tumor's specific profile, medical treatments or specialized, high-precision radiation therapy may be used after surgery to clear remaining cells.
  • Rehabilitation & Recovery: Specialized physical, occupational and speech therapy helps children regain strength, balance and confidence as they bounce back.
