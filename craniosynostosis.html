Craniosynostosis

What Is Craniosynostosis?

At birth, a baby's skull is not a single solid bone. Instead, it is made up of several individual bone plates held together by flexible, expandable seams called sutures. These soft seams allow the baby's head to squeeze safely through the birth canal and give the brain the room it needs to grow rapidly during the first few years of life.

Normally, these seams stay open until early childhood and slowly turn into solid bone once brain growth slows down.

Craniosynostosis occurs when one or more of these expansion seams close (fuse) prematurely before birth or in early infancy. When a seam closes too early, the skull can no longer expand naturally in that direction. To make room for the growing brain, the skull compensates by expanding sideways or upward in other open directions, leading to an atypical head shape.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

The main signs of craniosynostosis involve visible changes to the shape of the head and face, which are usually noticed shortly after birth or within the first few months:

Misshapen Skull

The head may look narrow and elongated, flattened on one side, triangular at the forehead or unusually short and tall, depending on which seam fused early.

A Hard, Raised Ridge

A firm, bony ridge felt along the line where the fused seam sits.

Disappearing or Abnormal "Soft Spot"

The soft spot (fontanelle) on top of the baby's head may feel closed too early or feel unusually firm and tight.

Facial Asymmetry

In certain types, the forehead, eyes, or cheekbones may appear misaligned or uneven.

Signs of High Pressure (Uncommon in early stages)

If multiple seams fuse early, it can restrict brain growth and cause pressure buildup, leading to irritability, sluggishness, unusual high-pitched crying or vomiting.

How Is It Discovered?

Diagnosing craniosynostosis early is important to plan the right treatment timeline while the skull bones are still soft and malleable.

  • Physical Examination: A pediatric neurosurgeon or craniofacial specialist can usually identify craniosynostosis simply by examining the head shape, feeling the skull seams and measuring head growth.
  • Three-Dimensional CT Scan (3D CT): A quick, specialized low-dose CT scan creates a clear 3D model of the skull bones. This confirms exactly which seams are closed and helps surgeons plan the procedure.
  • Eye Examinations: Checking the back of the baby's eyes (the optic nerve) to ensure there are no subtle signs of pressure buildup inside the skull.

How Is It Treated?

Surgery is the primary treatment for craniosynostosis. The main goals are to relieve any restriction on the growing brain and restore a natural head shape.

  • Minimally Invasive Endoscopic Surgery: Done in early infancy (typically under 3 to 6 months of age). A neurosurgeon uses a tiny camera and small incisions to release the fused seam. After surgery, the baby wears a custom-fitted cranial molding helmet for a few months to guide natural, rounded skull growth as the brain expands.
  • Open Cranial Vault Reconstruction: Performed for older infants (usually 6 months and older) or complex multi-seam cases. Surgeons carefully reshape and reposition the skull bones in a single procedure to create room for brain growth and correct the head contour.
  • Long-Term Monitoring: Children recover very well after repair, with team follow-ups monitoring head shape, vision and healthy developmental milestones as they grow.
