Moyamoya Disease

What Is Moyamoya Disease?

Imagine your brain’s primary blood supply as two main highway arteries (the internal carotid arteries) delivering fresh, oxygenated blood up into your head. Under normal conditions, these large arteries stay wide open, allowing a smooth, uninterrupted blood supply to every region of the brain.

Moyamoya disease is a rare, progressive blood vessel disorder in which these main supply arteries gradually narrow and eventually become completely blocked at the base of the brain.

To bypass these narrowed highways, your body attempts to compensate by growing a dense network of tiny, fragile collateral blood vessels. On diagnostic X-ray scans, this hazy tangle of tiny vessels looks like a "puff of smoke"—which translates to moyamoya in Japanese. Because these secondary backup vessels are tiny and fragile, they are prone to clotting, leaking, or rupturing, placing patients at risk for transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), ischemic strokes, or brain hemorrhages.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms differ between children and adults, primarily reflecting either reduced blood supply (ischemia) or fragile vessel rupture (bleeding):

Transient Ischemic Attacks (TIAs) or Mini-Strokes

Temporary episodes of weakness, numbness, or tingling in an arm or leg, slurred speech, or facial drooping that resolve within minutes or hours.

Hyperventilation-Triggered Symptoms

In children, mini-strokes or weakness are frequently triggered by crying, coughing, blowing on hot food, or strenuous exercise due to temporary changes in blood vessel carbon dioxide levels.

Seizures

Unprovoked electrical fits or muscle spasms caused by reduced blood flow and oxygen supply to localized brain tissue.

Frequent or Severe Headaches

Persistent, aching, or migraine-like headaches caused by abnormal blood pressure and dilation in small collateral vessels.

Sudden Brain Hemorrhage (More Common in Adults)

A sudden, severe "thunderclap" headache, vomiting, or loss of consciousness caused by the rupture of fragile, high-pressure collateral vessels.

How is it Discovered?

Detailed neurovascular imaging is essential to evaluate arterial narrowing and assess blood flow reserves throughout the brain:

  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA): The absolute gold-standard diagnostic test that clearly reveals the characteristic "puff of smoke" vessel tangle and maps out narrowing in the primary carotid arteries.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MRA: Detailed structural imaging and vessel mapping that identifies subtle past strokes, detects tiny bleeds, and tracks blood flow patterns without radiation.
  • CT Angiography (CTA): Rapid 3D vessel imaging used in emergency settings to quickly evaluate acute strokes or rule out brain hemorrhages.
  • Perfusion Imaging (SPECT / Perfusion MRI / CTP): Specialized scans that evaluate blood flow volume across different brain territories to determine which regions are starving for oxygen and need surgical revascularization.

How is it Treated?

Because Moyamoya disease is progressive, long-term management focuses on preventing strokes by surgically establishing new blood supply routes to the brain:

  • Direct Surgical Revascularization (STA-MCA Bypass): A precise neurosurgical procedure where a healthy scalp artery (the superficial temporal artery) is directly connected (stitched) to a brain surface artery to instantly restore blood flow.
  • Indirect Surgical Revascularization (Encephaloduroarteriomyosynangiosis or EDAMS): A procedure where blood-vessel-rich tissue (such as scalp muscle or membrane) is placed directly onto the surface of the brain, encouraging new blood vessels to naturally grow into the brain tissue over several months.
  • Combined (Hybrid) Revascularization: Combining direct bypass and indirect tissue placement to provide both immediate blood flow improvement and long-term collateral vessel growth.
  • Medical Management & Blood Thinners: Antiplatelet medications (such as low-dose aspirin) prescribed to lower the risk of blood clots forming in narrowed arterial channels.
