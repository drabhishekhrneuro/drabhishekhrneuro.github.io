Primary Central Nervous System (CNS) Lymphoma

What Is Primary CNS Lymphoma?

Primary Central Nervous System (CNS) Lymphoma is a rare, aggressive type of non-Hodgkin lymphoma that originates directly within the lymph tissue of the brain, spinal cord, meninges (brain lining), or eyes without spreading from elsewhere in the body.

Unlike systemic lymphomas that form in lymph nodes throughout the body, primary CNS lymphoma develops in the specialized environment of the central nervous system. White blood cells (lymphocytes) grow uncontrollably, forming mass lesions that press on healthy brain structures and interfere with normal neurological function.

Think of the central nervous system as a protected, high-security facility:

  • Localized Origins: The abnormal white blood cells develop within the brain tissue or its surrounding fluid pathways rather than traveling from distant lymph nodes.
  • Rapid Expansion: Because CNS lymphoma cells multiply quickly, tumor masses tend to expand rapidly, creating pressure within the rigid skull.
  • Targeted Impact: The tumor often forms in deep brain structures, affecting movement, speech, cognition, or personality depending on its exact location.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms often develop over a few weeks and reflect increasing pressure inside the skull or localized brain tissue strain:

Personality & Cognitive Changes

Unusual confusion, memory loss, apathy, mood swings, or difficulty concentrating as the tumor affects deep brain structures.

Focal Neurological Deficits

Progressive weakness, numbness, or paralysis on one side of the body, difficulty walking, or unsteady gait.

Persistent, Worsening Headaches

Deep, aching headaches caused by elevated pressure inside the skull, often worse in the morning or upon coughing.

Seizures

Unprovoked electrical fits or muscle twitching caused by tumor irritation of surrounding brain tissue.

Vision Disturbances

Blurred vision, floaters, double vision, or decreased sight if the lymphoma involves the eye or optic pathways (ocular lymphoma).

How is it Discovered?

Diagnosing primary CNS lymphoma requires specialized imaging and tissue analysis to confirm the diagnosis while ruling out infection or other brain tumors:

  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The primary diagnostic tool. Contrast-enhanced MRI reveals characteristic, intensely enhancing tumor masses in deep brain regions.
  • Stereotactic Needle Biopsy: A precise, minimally invasive neurosurgical procedure where a small needle is guided by 3D navigation to collect a tissue sample for microscopic diagnosis.
  • Lumbar Puncture (Spinal Tap): Analyzing a small sample of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) for microscopic cancer cells, protein levels, and flow cytometry markers.
  • Eye (Ophthalmological) Examination: A slit-lamp eye examination to detect microscopic lymphoma cells in the vitreous fluid of the eye.
  • Staging Workup (PET/CT Scans & Bone Marrow Biopsy): Scans of the chest, abdomen, pelvis, and body to confirm that the lymphoma is strictly limited to the central nervous system.

How is it Treated?

Unlike many other brain tumors where extensive surgical removal is key, CNS lymphoma is highly sensitive to non-surgical medical therapies:

  • High-Dose Methotrexate-Based Chemotherapy: The cornerstone of medical treatment. Specialized high-dose chemotherapy drugs designed to cross the blood-brain barrier to shrink and destroy tumor cells throughout the brain.
  • Targeted Immunotherapy: Monoclonal antibody drugs (such as Rituximab) combined with chemotherapy to specifically target and destroy abnormal B-lymphocytes.
  • Stereotactic Biopsy (Limited Surgical Role): Surgery is typically reserved for obtaining a diagnostic tissue biopsy rather than removing the entire tumor, as chemotherapy and radiation produce superior results with less tissue trauma.
  • Whole-Brain Radiation Therapy (WBRT): Highly focused radiation used in select cases to eliminate remaining tumor cells or as a secondary treatment for persistent disease.
  • Autologous Stem Cell Transplantation (ASCT): High-dose consolidation therapy followed by a rescue transplant of the patient's own healthy stem cells for eligible individuals to prevent recurrence.
