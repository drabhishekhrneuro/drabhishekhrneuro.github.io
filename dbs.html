Movement Disorder Surgery

Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)

What Is Deep Brain Stimulation?

Imagine your brain's motor control center as a complex orchestra. In movement disorders like Parkinson's disease, Essential Tremor, or Dystonia, key deep brain nuclei—such as the subthalamic nucleus (STN), globus pallidus interna (GPi), or ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus (VIM)—begin sending irregular, chaotic electrical noise. This abnormal signaling disrupts smooth motor commands, resulting in debilitating tremors, muscle rigidity, slowness of movement (bradykinesia), or involuntary muscle contractions.

When medications no longer provide consistent control or cause troublesome side effects (such as medication-induced dyskinesias), Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) offers an advanced, adjustable neurosurgical solution. Often referred to as a "pacemaker for the brain," DBS delivers targeted, high-frequency electrical pulses directly to these deep motor centers to override chaotic neural signals and restore balanced motor control.

Think of DBS as an adjustable dimmer switch that neutralizes electrical noise in the brain's motor circuits:

  • Reversible & Non-Destructive: Unlike older lesioning surgeries (such as thalamotomy or pallidotomy), DBS does not burn or permanently destroy brain tissue. The electrical stimulation can be adjusted, fine-tuned, or turned off at any time.
  • Fully Customized & Programmable: An implanted pulse generator (IPG) placed under the collarbone continuously delivers customized electrical impulses that are wirelessly fine-tuned in the clinic to match your evolving symptom needs.
  • Restores Daily Independence: Significantly reduces motor fluctuations, cuts down daily medication burdens, stabilizes tremors, and improves overall quality of life.

When Is Deep Brain Stimulation Recommended?

DBS is indicated for advanced movement disorders that fail to achieve stable control with optimized oral medications:

Parkinson's Disease Motor Complications

Severe motor fluctuations ("ON/OFF" shifts), debilitating tremors, or medication-induced involuntary writhing movements (dyskinesias) despite optimal levodopa timing.

Medication-Refractory Essential Tremor

Severe action or postural hand and arm tremors that impair eating, writing, drinking, or self-care, and fail to improve with beta-blockers or anti-seizure drugs.

Primary or Secondary Dystonia

Involuntary, sustained muscle contractions causing painful twisting postures (cervical dystonia, generalized dystonia) that do not respond to oral drugs or botulinum toxin injections.

Severe Medically Refractory OCD or Epilepsy

Select neurobehavioral or seizure conditions evaluated by specialized multidisciplinary boards when standard interventional pathways are exhausted.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Rigorous multidisciplinary evaluation and high-precision structural imaging guide target mapping:

  • Multidisciplinary Evaluation: In-depth assessment by a team including a movement disorder neurologist, functional neurosurgeon, neuropsychologist, and speech/physical therapists.
  • High-Resolution 3T Volumetric MRI: Merged with intraoperative CT or stereotactic frame coordinates to map sub-millimeter anatomical targets (STN, GPi, or VIM).
  • Levodopa Challenge Test (for Parkinson's): Formal motor scoring (UPDRS) in both the "OFF" (medication withheld) and "ON" states to confirm motor responsiveness prior to surgery.
  • Neuropsychological & Cognitive Screening: Evaluates memory, mood, and cognitive baseline to ensure deep brain stimulation can be tolerated safely without cognitive side effects.

What Happens During the Implant Surgery?

DBS implantation is typically performed in two stages (often on the same day or separated by a week):

  • Stage 1: Lead Placement (Brain Stage):
    • Frame / Frameless Navigation: A stereotactic head frame or custom micro-stereotactic fixture is secured, and fine-cut guidance scans are performed.
    • Microelectrode Recording (MER) & Testing: Performed awake (or under specialized asleep protocols). Tiny microelectrodes record real-time cellular signals down to the micron to verify the exact neural target.
    • Test Stimulation: Temporary test stimulation is delivered to confirm tremor arrest and ensure no unwanted side effects (such as facial pulling or speech slurring) before anchoring the permanent lead.
  • Stage 2: Pulse Generator Implantation (Chest Stage): Performed under general anesthesia. The brain lead is connected to an extension wire tunneled under the skin behind the ear and down to the upper chest, where it attaches to the small battery pack (neurostimulator) placed beneath the skin.

Post-Operative Recovery & Long-Term Care

Recovery involves initial wound healing, outpatient device programming, and gradual medication optimization:

  • Hospital Stay & Healing Phase (1 to 2 Days): Post-operative CT or MRI confirms exact lead placement. Incisional soreness on the scalp and chest is mild and managed with standard analgesics.
  • Initial Outpatient Programming (2 to 4 Weeks Post-Op): After surgical healing and resolution of micro-lesioning effects, the neurologist turns on the pulse generator and wirelessly adjusts voltage, frequency, and pulse width.
  • Medication Adjustment Period: As stimulation parameters are fine-tuned over several outpatient visits, Parkinson's medications are often gradually reduced under neurological supervision.
  • Long-Term Maintenance & Recharging: Regular clinical check-ups monitor battery status (rechargeable models require periodic home charging, while non-rechargeable batteries last 3 to 5 years before a simple outpatient battery change).
