Middle Meningeal Artery (MMA) Embolization for Chronic Subdural Hematoma

What Is Middle Meningeal Artery Embolization for Chronic SDH?

Imagine the surface of your brain covered by a protective membrane called the dura mater. Following a minor head bump or fall, tiny fragile blood vessels underneath this membrane can stretch and tear, slowly leaking blood into the subdural space. Over weeks or months, this collection forms a Chronic Subdural Hematoma (cSDH), wrapped in a fragile, inflamed outer membrane that continually forms leaky micro-capillaries—causing the clot to repeatedly re-bleed, expand, and compress the brain.

Middle Meningeal Artery (MMA) Embolization is an innovative, minimally invasive endovascular procedure that stops this cycle of chronic re-bleeding from the inside out. Rather than drilling or opening the skull, an endovascular specialist navigates an ultra-fine catheter from an artery in the wrist or groin directly into the primary blood vessel feeding the hematoma's outer membrane (the Middle Meningeal Artery) and seals it off using microscopic particles or liquid embolic agents.

Think of this procedure as turning off the main water valve feeding a slow, persistent leak:

  • Cutting Off the Supply: By blocking blood flow through the middle meningeal artery branches, the procedure starves the fragile, leaky outer membrane of its blood supply, stopping continuous micro-bleeding.
  • Incisionless & Keyhole Access: Performed entirely through a pinprick entry site in the wrist (radial artery) or groin (femoral artery), completely avoiding skull drilling (burr holes) or open craniotomy.
  • Promoting Natural Resorption: Once the active bleeding source is permanently shut down, the body's natural defense systems gradually absorb the existing blood clot over the following weeks to months.

When Is MMA Embolization Recommended for Chronic SDH?

MMA embolization is indicated for standalone treatment of mild-to-moderate chronic hematomas or as an adjunct to prevent high recurrence rates following surgical drainage:

Recurrent Chronic Subdural Hematomas

Patients who have previously undergone burr hole drainage or craniotomy but suffer from repeating blood accumulation due to persistent membrane re-bleeding.

Surgically Fragile or High-Risk Candidates

Elderly or medically complex patients (such as those on strong blood thinners or with severe cardiac conditions) where traditional skull surgery carries elevated risks.

Symptomatic Mild-to-Moderate cSDH

Patients presenting with persistent headaches, mild cognitive slowing, gait unsteadiness, or focal weakness where mass effect is moderate and does not require emergency surgical decompression.

Adjuvant Therapy During Surgical Evacuation

Performed immediately before or after traditional burr hole drainage to shut down membrane vascularity and drastically reduce the risk of postoperative hematoma recurrence.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Detailed neuro-imaging and multidisciplinary clinical review guide procedural planning:

  • Non-Contrast Brain CT Scan: Initial imaging establishing the exact thickness, density (hypodense vs. mixed-density indicating active re-bleeding), and midline mass effect of the subdural collection.
  • CT Angiography (CTA) or Diagnostic Cerebral Angiography (DSA): Advanced vascular mapping to confirm the anatomy of the external carotid artery, the branches of the middle meningeal artery, and rule out dangerous collateral connections to the eye (ophthalmic artery).
  • Coagulation & Blood Thinner Optimization: Evaluation of baseline blood clotting parameters, with temporary pauses or reversal of anticoagulant/antiplatelet medications as clinically guided.
  • Renal & Vascular Screening: Blood testing to verify normal kidney function for safe contrast dye clearance during fluoroscopy.

What Happens During the Procedure?

The procedure is carried out under local anesthesia with light sedation (or general anesthesia) in a specialized Hybrid Angiography Suite:

  • Stage 1: Vascular Access & Sheath Placement: Under local anesthesia, a tiny catheter sheath is placed into the radial (wrist) or femoral (groin) artery.
  • Stage 2: Selective Microcatheter Navigation: Guided by live 3D X-ray fluoroscopy (roadmapping), an ultra-fine microcatheter is steered into the external carotid artery and advanced selectively into the front (anterior) and back (posterior) branches of the middle meningeal artery.
  • Stage 3: Safety Check & Embolic Delivery: High-definition contrast injections confirm that no dangerous anatomical connections to the eye or cranial nerves exist. Microscopic embolic particles (such as PVA micro-beads) or liquid embolic materials are injected slowly to fill and block the distal arterial branches supplying the hematoma membrane.
  • Stage 4: Post-Embolization Angiography: Immediate follow-up angiograms verify complete stagnation of blood flow within the middle meningeal artery branches while keeping normal external carotid branches open.
  • Stage 5: Catheter Removal & Closure: Microcatheters are withdrawn, and a specialized arterial closure device or pressure band is applied to the wrist or groin access site.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery following MMA embolization is fast and comfortable due to the minimally invasive approach:

  • Short Hospital Stay (24 to 48 Hours): Patients are monitored overnight in a specialized ward or step-down unit with frequent neurological assessments and vital sign checks.
  • Puncture Site Care & Early Mobilization: Wrist access allows immediate walking and sitting up post-procedure. Puncture sites heal rapidly over a couple of days without stitches.
  • Gradual Symptom Resolution: As the active bleeding source remains closed, headaches, weakness, and confusion improve progressively over subsequent weeks as the brain absorbs the trapped blood.
  • Follow-Up Serial CT Scans: Routine follow-up non-contrast brain CT scans are scheduled at 1, 3, and 6 months post-procedure to monitor progressive shrinkage and complete resolution of the chronic hematoma.
