Dural Arteriovenous Fistula (DAVF)

What Is a Dural Arteriovenous Fistula?

Imagine your brain’s blood vessels as two separate fluid systems: high-pressure arterial pipes supplying oxygenated blood to the brain and low-pressure venous channels (the dural sinuses) built into the brain’s outer protective lining (the dura) to drain blood back to the heart.

A Dural Arteriovenous Fistula (DAVF) is an abnormal direct connection (a short circuit) between one or more arteries in the dura and a dural venous sinus or cortical vein, completely skipping normal capillary beds.

Because there are no capillaries to absorb and slow down arterial pressure, high-pressure blood rushes directly into the low-pressure venous system. Over time, this causes blood to back up (venous congestion), raising pressure within the brain's drainage pathways and increasing the risk of brain swelling, neurological symptoms, or hemorrhage.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms depend heavily on the location of the fistula and whether high-pressure blood is backing up into delicate cortical brain veins:

Pulsatile Tinnitus

A rhythmic "whooshing," thumping, or humming noise in one or both ears that beats in exact sync with your heartbeat.

Eye Symptoms & Redness

Bulging of the eye, redness, eye pain, or double vision if the fistula forms near the base of the skull or cavernous sinus.

Persistent or Localized Headaches

Dull, aching, or throbbing headaches caused by increased pressure in the dural sinuses or localized venous congestion.

Progressive Neurological Deficits

Gradual weakness, numbness, trouble walking, memory problems, or speech difficulties caused by high venous pressure straining adjacent brain tissue.

Seizures or Sudden Severe Hemorrhage

Unprovoked seizures or a sudden "thunderclap" headache with vomiting and confusion if high-pressure blood causes a vein to rupture.

How is it Discovered?

Precise diagnostic imaging is essential to classify the fistula's drainage pattern and evaluate the risk of bleeding:

  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA): The absolute gold-standard diagnostic procedure that maps out every feeding artery, locates the exact fistulous connection, and determines if blood is dangerously backing up into cortical brain veins (cortical venous reflux).
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MRA: Detailed structural imaging that identifies swollen venous channels, detects subtle brain tissue swelling, and screens for microscopic past bleeds.
  • CT Scan & CT Angiography (CTA): Rapid imaging used to quickly detect fresh bleeding, evaluate surrounding skull bones, and visualize enlarged blood vessel channels.

How is it Treated?

Treatment focuses on permanently closing the abnormal direct connection to eliminate high pressure in the venous system and prevent bleeding:

  • Endovascular Embolization: The primary and most effective treatment option, where micro-catheters are guided through blood vessels to seal off the abnormal connection using specialized liquid embolic agents (such as Onyx or Squid) or platinum coils.
  • Microsurgical Disconnection: A direct neurosurgical procedure where a surgeon creates a small window in the skull to physically isolate, disconnect, or clip the fistula when endovascular access is not feasible or safe.
  • Stereotactic Radiosurgery (Gamma Knife / CyberKnife): Highly focused radiation treatment utilized for small, low-risk fistulas in difficult locations to gradually thicken and close off the abnormal connections over 1 to 3 years.
  • Observation & Monitoring: For low-risk DAVFs that do not exhibit dangerous cortical venous drainage and cause minimal symptoms, careful clinical and imaging follow-up may be advised.
