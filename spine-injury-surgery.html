Spine Surgery

Spine Injury & Traumatic Fracture Surgery

What Is Spine Injury Surgery?

Imagine your spinal column as a central load-bearing pillar that houses and protects your spinal cord—the main communications cable connecting your brain to the rest of your body. When high-energy trauma occurs, such as a motor vehicle collision, a severe fall, or a sports impact, the structural vertebrae, disc spaces, or supporting ligaments can fracture, tear, or dislocate.

Traumatic spine injuries can cause unstable burst fractures, fracture-dislocations, or bone fragments that drive into the spinal canal, directly squeezing or tearing the spinal cord and surrounding nerve roots. This compression risks immediate or progressive paralysis, loss of sensation, and chronic disability.

Spine Injury Surgery is an urgent specialized neurosurgical intervention designed to decompress compressed spinal nerves and the spinal cord, realign fractured or dislocated vertebrae, and permanently lock the injured segment into solid alignment using high-strength titanium hardware and bone grafts.

Think of spine injury surgery as emergency structural repair and nerve decompression:

  • Emergency Cord Decompression: Removes bone shrapnel, disc fragments, or hematomas crushing the spinal cord, halting progressive nerve damage and creating the optimal biological environment for potential nerve recovery.
  • Anatomical Realignment: Pulls dislocated, slipped, or fractured vertebrae back into their natural anatomical curvature (restoring lordosis or kyphosis balance).
  • Rigid Internal Stabilization: Anchors titanium screws, rods, or replacement cages across the injured levels, instantly stabilizing the spine so patients can safely sit, stand, and begin rehabilitation without risking further nerve injury.

When Is Spine Injury Surgery Recommended?

Surgical intervention is indicated when traumatic spinal injuries compromise neurological function, cause structural instability, or fail conservative bracing:

Spinal Cord Compression & Neurological Deficits

Sudden weakness, loss of movement (paraplegia or tetraplegia), severe numbness, or loss of bowel and bladder control following a traumatic impact.

Unstable Spinal Fractures & Dislocations

Fractures involving multiple structural columns of the spine (such as burst fractures or flexion-distraction injuries) where the spine cannot support normal body weight.

Torn Spinal Ligaments & Joint Disruption

Complete rupture of posterior ligament complexes or facet joint dislocations that prevent natural bone healing and lead to progressive deformities.

Severe Intractable Pain & Progressive Deformity

Severe local pain and worsening spinal collapse (kyphosis) caused by acute vertebral collapse that fails non-surgical collar or brace immobilization.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Emergency trauma protocols and multi-planar diagnostic imaging guide surgical stabilization:

  • Whole-Spine Fine-Cut CT Scan (with 3D Reconstructions): The primary emergency trauma imaging study used to map bone fracture lines, pedicle integrity, canal compromise percentage, and subluxation.
  • High-Resolution Spine MRI: Assesses soft-tissue structures, evaluating spinal cord edema, contusions, traumatic disc herniations, and ligamentous disruption (Posterior Ligamentous Complex / PLC).
  • Hemodynamic & Neuro-Resuscitation Optimization: Maintaining mean arterial pressure (MAP) goals to ensure optimal blood flow and oxygen delivery to the injured spinal cord prior to surgery.
  • Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM) Setup: Preparing real-time motor (MEP) and sensory (SSEP) electrical signal tracking to safeguard nerve function throughout reduction and hardware placement.

What Happens During the Surgery?

The surgery is executed under general anesthesia through precise microsurgical and orthopedic stabilization phases:

  • Stage 1: Position & Fluoroscopic Alignment: The patient is carefully log-rolled face-down (prone) or side-lying on a specialized radio-lucent frame. Continuous X-ray fluoroscopy confirms fracture alignment.
  • Stage 2: Surgical Access & Exposure: A central incision (open or percutaneous keyhole depending on injury complexity) exposes the damaged spinal levels while protecting healthy surrounding muscles.
  • Stage 3: Decompression of the Spinal Cord: Working under high-magnification operating optics, the surgeon performs a laminectomy or corpectomy to carefully clear driven bone fragments, hematomas, or ruptured disc pieces pressing on the dural sheath.
  • Stage 4: Fracture Reduction & Rigid Fixation: Dislocated spinal joints are unlocked and reduced back into place. Polyaxial titanium pedicle screws are anchored into solid vertebrae above and below the fracture level and locked with heavy contoured rods.
  • Stage 5: Bone Grafting & Layered Closure: Joint surfaces are prepared and packed with autologous or synthetic bone graft to foster permanent biological fusion. Deep drains are placed, and muscle and skin layers are closed securely.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Post-operative care focuses on spinal protection, intensive neuro-rehabilitation, and long-term functional recovery:

  • ICU / HDU Monitoring (1 to 5 Days): Multi-system trauma care including close blood pressure support, respiratory monitoring, wound drain management, and frequent neurological evaluations.
  • Early Physical & Occupational Rehabilitation: Mobility begins as soon as the construct is verified stable. Physical therapists assist with sitting, transfers, standing, or specialized wheelchair/gait training.
  • External Supportive Bracing: Depending on fracture location and bone quality, a custom rigid neck collar (Miami J) or thoracolumbar brace (TLSO) may be worn for 6 to 12 weeks during early bone healing.
  • Long-Term Fusion & Neurological Surveillance: Serial X-rays and CT scans at 3, 6, and 12 months track solid biological bone fusion and hardware stability while nerve recovery progresses over several months.
