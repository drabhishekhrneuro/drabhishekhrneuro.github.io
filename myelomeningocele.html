Spina Bifida & Myelomeningocele

What Is Spina Bifida?

Think of the spine as a protective armour made of bones (vertebrae) that wraps around a delicate bundle of wires—the spinal cord. This wire bundle carries signals back and forth between your brain and the rest of your body, controlling how you walk, feel, and use the bathroom.

During the first month of pregnancy, this spinal armor develops by folding closed like a zipper. Spina Bifida happens when a small section of that zipper fails to close completely.

While some mild forms of Spina Bifida leave only a tiny hidden gap in the bone with no symptoms at all, Myelomeningocele is one of the most serious forms. Here the gap is wide enough that a small section of the spinal cord and its fluid bubble outward, forming a visible, delicate sac on the baby's back at birth.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because the spinal cord acts like the body’s main electrical cable, a gap in the armor affects how signals reach the lower body:

A Visible Sac on the Back

A soft, fluid-filled pouch visible on the baby's spine right at birth.

Leg Weakness or Loss of Movement

Depending on how high up the gap is on the spine, the baby may have weak leg movements or reduced feeling in their feet and legs.

Bladder & Bowel Control Changes

The nerves that control the bowel and bladder start from the lower spinal cord. When those nerve signals are disrupted, the body may have trouble storing or releasing urine smoothly.

Fluid Buildup in the Brain (Hydrocephalus)

The brain and spinal cord share the same circulating fluid. An opening in the spine can affect how this fluid drains, leading to extra fluid in the brain.

How is it Discovered?

Spina Bifida is often picked up well before the baby arrives, giving parents and doctors time to prepare:

  • Routine Pregnancy Blood Tests:A simple blood test during the second trimester checks for specific proteins that act as early clues.
  • Pregnancy Ultrasound Scans: Detailed ultrasound scans allow doctors to look closely at the baby’s spine and check how fluid is moving in the brain.
  • Post-Birth Examination & Scans:After birth, gentle physical exams and quick, harmless imaging scans (like an MRI or ultrasound) show exact details of the spinal nerves so the care team can plan the safest repair.

How is it Treated?

Treating Myelomeningocele is all about protecting the delicate nerves and helping the child live an active and fulfilling life:

  • Early Repair Surgery: Within the first 24 to 48 hours of life, a neurosurgeon carefully tucks the spinal cord safely back inside its protective space and closes the skin over it. Think of this like repairing a tear in an electrical wire’s insulation so it stays safe and clean.
  • Managing Brain Fluid Pressure: If extra fluid builds up in the brain, doctors can place a tiny tube (called a VP shunt) or create a small natural bypass inside the brain (an ETV procedure) to keep fluid draining normally.
  • Long-Term Team Support:As the child grows, a supportive team of specialists—including pediatric neurosurgeons, physical therapists and kidney/bladder doctors—work together to keep them moving, active and independent.
