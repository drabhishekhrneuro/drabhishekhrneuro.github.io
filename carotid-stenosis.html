Carotid Artery Stenosis

What Is Carotid Artery Stenosis?

Imagine the carotid arteries in your neck as two main water supply pipes bringing clean, oxygen-rich blood directly from your heart up to your brain. Under normal conditions, these arteries are smooth, wide, and flexible, ensuring a steady and uninterrupted flow of blood.

Carotid artery stenosis occurs when fatty deposits, cholesterol, and calcium—collectively known as plaque—build up along the inner walls of these major neck arteries (atherosclerosis).

As this plaque buildup thickens over time, it gradually narrows the artery channel, restricting healthy blood flow to the brain. Furthermore, the surface of the plaque can become rough or crack, encouraging small blood clots to form that can break loose and travel straight into the brain, causing a transient ischemic attack (TIA) or a major ischemic stroke.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

In its early stages, carotid stenosis is often "silent" and causes no symptoms until blood flow to the brain is suddenly reduced or blocked:

Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA or "Mini-Stroke")

Temporary warning signs that appear suddenly and resolve within a few minutes to hours, serving as a critical warning for a future major stroke.

Sudden Facial Drooping or Asymmetry

Numbness, weakness, or noticeable sagging on one side of the face when attempting to smile.

One-Sided Body Weakness or Numbness

Sudden loss of strength, heaviness, or clumsiness in an arm or leg, typically on one side of the body.

Speech and Language Difficulties

Sudden trouble speaking, slurred words, or difficulty understanding what others are saying.

Temporary Vision Loss ("Amaurosis Fugax")

A sudden, painless loss of vision in one eye, often described as a dark shade or curtain pulled down over the field of sight.

How is it Discovered?

Early screening and non-invasive vascular imaging allow doctors to evaluate the exact percentage of blockage and assess stroke risk:

  • Carotid Duplex Ultrasound: A painless, non-invasive soundwave scan that measures blood flow velocity and visualizes the thickness of plaque inside the neck arteries.
  • CT Angiography (CTA): A fast, high-resolution scan utilizing contrast dye to create detailed 3D maps of the neck and brain blood vessels.
  • Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA): Specialized MRI imaging that evaluates vessel narrowing, assesses plaque stability, and checks brain tissue for past silent mini-strokes without using X-ray radiation.
  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA): The precise gold-standard diagnostic procedure where a fine catheter injects contrast dye to capture real-time X-ray movies of carotid blood flow prior to complex intervention.

How is it Treated?

Treatment strategy depends on the severity of arterial narrowing (percentage of stenosis) and whether you have experienced prior stroke symptoms:

  • Medical Management & Lifestyle Control: For mild-to-moderate or asymptomatic narrowing, treatment includes antiplatelet medications (such as aspirin or clopidogrel), cholesterol-lowering statins, blood pressure management, and smoking cessation.
  • Carotid Endarterectomy (CEA): A traditional open surgical procedure where a vascular surgeon or neurosurgeon makes an incision in the neck, opens the narrowed carotid artery, and directly removes the plaque lining to restore full blood flow.
  • Carotid Artery Stenting (CAS): A minimally invasive procedure where a fine catheter is guided through the blood vessels to place a metal mesh stent and inflate a balloon, pressing the plaque against the artery wall and keeping the vessel propped open.
  • Transcarotid Artery Revascularization (TCAR): An advanced hybrid procedure that delivers a stent directly into the neck artery while temporarily reversing blood flow away from the brain to prevent micro-clots from traveling during placement.
