Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS)

What Is Stereotactic Radiosurgery?

Imagine your brain as a dense, high-precision electronic network. When a deep-seated tumor, vascular malformation, or functional pain focus develops, traditional open surgical removal may carry high risks of damaging surrounding critical brain pathways. Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS)—delivered via specialized technologies like Gamma Knife, CyberKnife, or dedicated Linear Accelerators (LINAC)—is an advanced, non-invasive neurosurgical treatment that delivers hundreds of micro-targeted beams of high-dose radiation to a precise target deep within the brain without making a single surgical incision.

Despite the word "surgery" in its name, SRS involves no scalpel, open craniotomy, or physical blood loss. Instead, sophisticated 3D computer navigation converges hundreds of low-dose radiation beams onto a single, sub-millimeter target focal point. While each individual beam passes safely through surrounding healthy brain tissue without causing damage, the intersection point receives a concentrated, therapeutic dose of radiation sufficient to destroy tumor cells, close abnormal blood vessels, or interrupt painful nerve signals.

Think of Stereotactic Radiosurgery as a highly precise, non-invasive beam that treats brain lesions without an incision:

  • Sub-Millimeter Precision: Uses advanced 3D stereotactic navigation and real-time imaging to sculpt radiation doses to the exact shape of the lesion, sparing healthy brain tissue and critical nerves.
  • Incisionless Outpatient Care: Performed entirely without open skull entry, general anesthesia, or prolonged hospital stays, making it ideal for deep-seated or surgically high-risk lesions.
  • Multiple Interventional Modalities: Can be delivered as a single high-dose session (Stereotactic Radiosurgery) or divided into 2 to 5 smaller daily doses (Fractionated Stereotactic Radiotherapy / SRT) for larger targets near critical structures.

When Is Stereotactic Radiosurgery Recommended?

SRS is indicated for a wide array of benign and malignant brain tumors, vascular malformations, and functional neurological conditions:

Brain Metastases & Primary Brain Tumors

Single or multiple metastatic brain tumors (from lung, breast, or melanoma sources), benign meningiomas, acoustic neuromas (vestibular schwannomas), or pituitary adenomas.

Arteriovenous Malformations (AVMs)

Complex, high-flow abnormal tangles of blood vessels in the brain that carry a risk of rupture and bleeding, especially those located in deep or eloquent brain regions.

Medically Refractory Trigeminal Neuralgia

Severe, electric shock-like facial pain caused by trigeminal nerve irritation that fails to improve with medications or where microvascular decompression surgery carries high medical risks.

Residual or Recurrent Tumors

Persistent or growing tumor tissue following prior incomplete open surgical resection or deep-seated lesions inaccessible by traditional open surgery.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Meticulous multi-modal imaging and computer-assisted dose planning form the foundation of successful treatment:

  • High-Resolution 3T Volumetric MRI & CT Scans: Fine-cut structural MRI sequences (T1 contrast, constructive interference in steady state) are fused with stereotactic CT scans to map target boundaries in 3D space.
  • Stereotactic Frame or Mask Immobilization: A lightweight stereotactic frame (secured under local anesthesia) or a custom-molded thermoplastic mesh face mask is used to keep the head perfectly still down to the millimeter during treatment.
  • Multidisciplinary Dosimetry & Radiation Planning: A team consisting of a functional neurosurgeon, radiation oncologist, and medical physicist designs a customized 3D dose plan that conforms tightly to the tumor while protecting nearby structures like optic nerves and the brainstem.
  • Quality Assurance & Safety Calibration: Phantoms and software algorithms verify beam trajectory, couch movement parameters, and dosage intensity prior to patient positioning.

What Happens During the Procedure?

The treatment is conducted on an outpatient basis and typically takes between 30 minutes to a few hours depending on target complexity:

  • Stage 1: Patient Positioning & Registration: The patient lies comfortably on the treatment couch. The stereotactic frame or custom thermoplastic mask is fixed to the table to ensure complete position accuracy.
  • Stage 2: Image-Guided Alignment Verification: Integrated real-time X-ray or CT imaging verifies patient positioning, matching live anatomical coordinates with the 3D computer plan.
  • Stage 3: Precision Beam Delivery: The automated machine (Gamma Knife, CyberKnife, or LINAC) rotates around the patient's head, delivering targeted radiation beams from multiple precise angles while the patient rests comfortably.
  • Stage 4: Real-Time Patient Monitoring: Closed-circuit cameras and continuous intercom communication allow the medical team to monitor the patient throughout the entire delivery process.
  • Stage 5: Frame/Mask Removal & Completion: Once beam delivery is complete, the treatment table moves back, the frame or mask is removed, and pin sites (if a frame was used) are cleaned and dressed.

Post-Operative Recovery & Long-Term Care

Recovery is rapid, with minimal downtime and a swift return to daily activities:

  • Same-Day Outpatient Discharge: Patients are observed briefly in the outpatient suite and usually return home the same day. Normal daily routines and light activity can typically resume within 24 to 48 hours.
  • Management of Mild Acute Side Effects: Transient mild fatigue, temporary headache, or pin-site soreness (if a rigid frame was used) are managed easily with simple oral medications.
  • Gradual Therapeutic Response: Radiation works by disrupting the DNA of target cells. Tumors shrink or stabilize slowly over months, AVM blood vessels gradually close over 1 to 3 years, and trigeminal neuralgia pain typically subsides over weeks to months.
  • Long-Term Neuro-Imaging Surveillance: Follow-up high-resolution MRI scans are scheduled at regular intervals (typically 3, 6, and 12 months) to monitor target control, evaluate tumor regression, and confirm long-term success.
