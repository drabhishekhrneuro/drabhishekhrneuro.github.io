Surgery for Spinal Vascular Malformations

What Is Surgery for Spinal Vascular Malformations?

Imagine your spinal cord as a complex electrical network that relies on a delicate, low-pressure capillary system to receive oxygenated blood and drain waste. A spinal vascular malformation is an abnormal, direct connection between high-pressure arteries and low-pressure veins along or within the spinal cord, bypassing the normal protective capillary network. Common types include Spinal Dural Arteriovenous Fistulas (SDAVFs) and Spinal Arteriovenous Malformations (AVMs).

This abnormal high-pressure arterial flow surges directly into the venous system, causing severe venous congestion, swelling (edema), reduced oxygen delivery (ischemia), or even sudden bleeding (hemorrhage) within the spinal cord. Over time, this leads to progressive leg weakness, numbness, balance problems, and loss of bowel or bladder control.

Surgery for spinal vascular malformations—often performed as microsurgical obliteration or excision—is a highly specialized neurosurgical procedure aimed at permanently disconnecting the abnormal arterial feeder vessels (fistula point) or completely removing the vascular malformation to restore normal spinal cord blood flow dynamics.

Think of this procedure as repairing a high-pressure plumbing leak around the spinal cord:

  • Direct Fistula Ligation: Micro-clipping or cutting the abnormal connection point between the feeding artery and draining vein immediately stops high-pressure backflow into the spinal cord's delicate veins.
  • Cord Decompression & Ischemia Relief: Restoring normal low-pressure venous drainage halts progressive spinal cord swelling and improves local oxygen delivery, preventing irreversible neurological injury.
  • Microsurgical Precision: Performed under high-power operating microscope magnification with real-time fluorescent angiography, ensuring the abnormal fistula is sealed while protecting every normal vessel nourishing the spinal cord.

When Is Surgical Treatment Recommended?

Surgical intervention is indicated when a spinal vascular malformation causes progressive neurological decline, severe spinal cord swelling, or carries a high risk of hemorrhage:

Progressive Spinal Myelopathy

Gradually worsening leg weakness, heaviness while walking, gait instability, or leg stiffness caused by chronic venous congestion and spinal cord swelling.

Sensory Disturbances & Pain

Progressive numbness, burning sensations, or tingling starting in the feet and ascending up the legs, often accompanied by chronic mid-back or lower-back pain.

Bowel or Bladder Dysfunction

Emerging difficulties with urinary urgency, hesitation, incontinence, or loss of bowel control resulting from chronic spinal cord congestion.

Sudden Severe Neurological Deficits (Hemorrhage)

Sudden onset of severe back pain accompanied by rapid leg paralysis or numbness caused by rupture of an intramedullary or perimedullary spinal AVM.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Advanced diagnostic neuro-angiography and structural imaging are vital for mapping the exact vascular anatomy prior to surgery:

  • High-Resolution Contrast Spine MRI & MR Angiography (MRA): Detects spinal cord swelling (T2 hyperintensity), abnormal dilated venous plexuses (flow voids), and pinpoints the approximate level of the lesion.
  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA - Spinal Angiogram): The absolute gold standard diagnostic test. Sequential catheterization of segmental spinal arteries precisely identifies the exact feeding artery, fistula site, and venous drainage pattern.
  • Multidisciplinary Evaluation (Endovascular vs. Microsurgical): Determining whether endovascular embolization (liquid embolic agents) or open microsurgical ligation offers the safest, most definitive cure.
  • Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM) Setup: Continuous tracking of motor (MEP) and sensory (SSEP) electrical pathways throughout the operation to protect spinal cord function.

What Happens During the Surgery?

The operation is performed under general anesthesia utilizing microsurgical standards, intraoperative angiography, and real-time nerve monitoring:

  • Stage 1: Position & Micro-Access: The patient is positioned face-down (prone) on a specialized spinal frame. X-ray guidance (fluoroscopy) confirms the exact spinal level, and a targeted posterior midline incision is made.
  • Stage 2: Laminectomy & Dural Exposure: A localized laminectomy (removal of the bony roof of the spinal canal) is performed over the target level. The protective dural sheath is opened under microscopic visualization to expose the spinal cord and abnormal blood vessels.
  • Stage 3: Intraoperative Indocyanine Green (ICG) Videoangiography: An intravenous fluorescent dye (ICG) is injected, illuminating the blood vessels under the microscope within seconds to confirm the precise arterial feeder and draining vein.
  • Stage 4: Microsurgical Ligation & Obliteration:
    • For Spinal DFistulas (SDAVF): The abnormal arterializing vein as it pierces the dura is isolated, micro-clipped, and coagulated, instantly stopping abnormal high-pressure flow.
    • For Spinal AVMs: Feeding arteries are meticulously coagulated and severed using micro-instruments, allowing safe excision of the vascular nidus while preserving native spinal cord vessels.
  • Stage 5: Repeat ICG Flow Verification & Watertight Closure: Repeat ICG videoangiography confirms total obliteration of the malformation and normal blood flow through surrounding spinal cord vessels. The dural sheath is closed watertight, and muscle and skin layers are closed over a temporary drain.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery focuses on spinal cord healing, rehabilitation, and long-term diagnostic confirmation:

  • Post-Op ICU / HDU Care (1 to 2 Days): Close monitoring of blood pressure, fluid balance, and frequent neurological exams to manage post-obliteration spinal cord pressure dynamics. Physical therapy initiates early bedside mobilization.
  • Spinal Cord Swallowing & Healing: As the abnormal high venous pressure drops, spinal cord swelling gradually recedes. Leg pain and sensory symptoms often improve early, while motor strength recovers over weeks to months.
  • Targeted Rehabilitation: Dedicated physical and occupational therapy programs help patients rebuild leg strength, optimize gait stability, and manage bladder or bowel rehabilitation.
  • Follow-Up Spinal Angiography (DSA): A post-operative spinal angiogram is performed at structured intervals (e.g., during hospital stay or at 3 to 6 months) to confirm 100% complete occlusion and cure of the malformation.
