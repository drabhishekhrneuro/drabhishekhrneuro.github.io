Carpal Tunnel Release (Open & Endoscopic)

What Is Carpal Tunnel Release?

Imagine your wrist as a busy entryway where nine flexor tendons and one major nerve—the median nerve—pass from your forearm into your hand through a tight passageway called the carpal tunnel. The floor and sides of this tunnel are formed by rigid wrist bones, while the roof is sealed by a thick, unyielding fibrous band known as the transverse carpal ligament.

When repetitive wrist movement, joint inflammation, fluid retention, or structural narrowing increases pressure inside this unyielding corridor, the median nerve gets squeezed and deprived of blood flow. This causes progressive numbness, tingling, burning pain, and weakness across the thumb, index, middle, and ring fingers—a condition known as Carpal Tunnel Syndrome.

Carpal Tunnel Release is a highly effective peripheral nerve procedure designed to permanently relieve pressure on the median nerve. By carefully incising and dividing the transverse carpal ligament, the neurosurgeon expands the tunnel's volume, restoring vital blood supply to the median nerve and halting progressive hand dysfunction.

Think of carpal tunnel release as opening a crowded, high-pressure corridor to relieve trapped nerve compression:

  • Direct Pressure Relief: Dividing the tight transverse carpal ligament immediately expands the carpal tunnel space, stopping mechanical friction and ischemic stress on the median nerve.
  • Surgical Approaches (Open vs. Endoscopic): Can be performed through a small mini-open palm incision or via minimally invasive endoscopic technique (using a tiny camera and micro-blade through a keyhole wrist crease puncture).
  • Rapid Functional Recovery: Performed as a brief outpatient procedure under local or regional anesthesia, offering fast relief from nocturnal hand numbness and restoring grip comfort.

When Is Carpal Tunnel Release Recommended?

Surgical intervention is indicated when median nerve compression causes persistent, disruptive symptoms that fail conservative management:

Persistent Numbness & Nocturnal Tingling

Waking up frequently at night with painful "pins and needles" or dead numbness in the thumb, index, and middle fingers that requires shaking out the hand for relief.

Thenar Muscle Atrophy & Hand Weakness

Noticeable flattening or shrinking of the muscular pad at the base of the thumb (thenar wasting), resulting in clumsiness or dropping objects regularly.

Constant Burning Pain & Sensory Loss

Continuous numbness or burning pain radiating from the wrist into the fingers or up the forearm that impairs fine motor tasks like buttoning shirts or gripping a pen.

Failure of Non-Surgical Therapies

Symptoms that persist or worsen despite weeks of nocturnal wrist splinting, activity modification, ergonomic adjustments, or steroid injections.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Clinical nerve assessment and objective electrodiagnostic testing confirm the diagnosis prior to surgery:

  • Electrodiagnostic Nerve Studies (EMG / NCV): Diagnostic gold standard that measures electrical signal conduction velocity across the wrist, quantifying the severity of median nerve compression.
  • Provocative Clinical Examination: Positive clinical tests including Phalen's wrist flexion test, Tinel's sign (tapping over the median nerve), and carpal compression testing.
  • High-Resolution Wrist Ultrasound or MRI (Select Cases): Evaluates median nerve cross-sectional area, swelling, or structural space-occupying lesions within the carpal tunnel.
  • Outpatient Anesthetic Planning: Prepared under simple local anesthesia (WALANT—Wide Awake Local Anesthesia No Tourniquet) or intravenous regional block for maximum comfort.

What Happens During the Surgery?

The procedure takes approximately 15 to 20 minutes in an outpatient operating room setting:

  • Stage 1: Position & Skin Preparation: The patient is positioned comfortably, and the hand and wrist are thoroughly disinfected and draped in a sterile field. Local anesthetic is infiltrated into the palm and wrist.
  • Stage 2: Surgical Access Corridor:
    • Mini-Open Approach: A small, vertical 2 to 3 cm skin incision is made along the natural crease of the palm.
    • Endoscopic Approach: A tiny keyhole puncture (approx. 1 cm) is made at the wrist crease, and an endoscope equipped with a high-definition camera is inserted beneath the transverse carpal ligament.
  • Stage 3: Micro-Division of Transverse Carpal Ligament: Working under direct visualization or endoscopic camera guidance, the surgeon precisely cuts the thick transverse carpal ligament from end to end, fully unroofing the tunnel.
  • Stage 4: Nerve Inspection & Decompression Verification: The median nerve is directly inspected to confirm complete release of all compressive fibrous bands and restore capillary blood flow.
  • Stage 5: Skin Closure & Soft Dressing: The skin incision is closed with delicate sutures, and a soft protective bandage or light splint is applied over the wrist—leaving the fingers free to move immediately.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery emphasizes immediate gentle finger motion, proper wound healing, and gradual return to full activity:

  • Same-Day Outpatient Discharge: Patients return home shortly after the procedure. Early night-time tingling and pain often improve within the first 24 to 48 hours.
  • Immediate Gentle Finger Movement: Patients are encouraged to move and flex their fingers right away to promote blood circulation and prevent tendon stiffness inside the wrist.
  • Sutures & Hand Care (10 to 14 Days): The wrist dressing is kept clean and dry; skin sutures are removed in the clinic around 2 weeks post-op.
  • Gradual Activity Resumption: Light daily hand use (eating, typing) resumes within days, while heavy pushing, pulling, or heavy lifting is restricted for 3 to 4 weeks to allow palm tissues to heal securely. Complete nerve recovery and grip strength rebuilding continue over several months.
