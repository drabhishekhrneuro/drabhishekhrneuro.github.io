Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy (ETV)

What Is an Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy (ETV)?

Imagine your brain's internal fluid pathways as an interconnected chain of lakes and rivers. Deep inside the brain, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is constantly produced to cushion brain tissue and remove waste. When a physical blockage arises along one of the narrow connecting channels between these internal chambers (ventricles), fluid pools behind the obstruction, causing obstructive hydrocephalus.

Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy (ETV) is a minimally invasive neurosurgical procedure that creates a natural internal bypass within the brain's fluid pathways, allowing CSF to flow around the blockage without needing a permanent mechanical shunt implant.

Think of ETV as creating a natural relief channel for trapped fluid:

  • Internal Fluid Bypass: A high-definition endoscope (a tiny camera) is guided into the brain's third ventricle, where a millimeter-sized hole is made in its floor. This allows CSF to drain directly into the natural subarachnoid spaces surrounding the base of the brain, where it can be absorbed naturally into the bloodstream.
  • Shunt-Free Solution: Because ETV restores internal fluid circulation, it eliminates the long-term risks associated with artificial hardware, such as mechanical shunt blockages, valve malfunctions, or tubing infections.
  • Key Indications: Highly effective for obstructive hydrocephalus caused by aqueductal stenosis, posterior fossa tumours, colloid cysts, tectal plate lesions, or selective post-hemorrhagic conditions.

When Is ETV Recommended?

ETV is indicated when diagnostic imaging confirms a structural blockage in the CSF pathways while the brain's outer absorption pathways remain healthy and functional:

Congenital or Acquired Aqueductal Stenosis

Narrowing of the Aqueduct of Sylvius—the tight channel connecting the third and fourth ventricles—preventing normal CSF outflow.

Obstructive Brain Tumours or Cysts

When mass lesions (such as pineal region tumours, tectal gliomas, or colloid cysts) press against and seal off CSF passages.

Shunt Malfunction or Infection Alternative

In patients with existing VP shunts who experience repeated hardware failures, ETV may be performed to convert them to a hardware-free, natural fluid pathway.

Posterior Fossa Lesions with Hydrocephalus

Relieving critical intracranial pressure caused by fluid blockage before or during treatment of cerebellar or posterior skull base lesions.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Thorough preoperative imaging and anatomical mapping are essential to ensure that the patient's internal anatomy is suitable for a successful ventriculostomy:

  • High-Resolution 3T Brain MRI (CINE CSF Flow): Specialized dynamic MRI sequences map CSF velocity, evaluate third ventricle floor geometry, confirm internal blockage, and verify that subarachnoid absorption pathways are open.
  • Neuronavigation CT / MRI Fusion: 3D imaging data loaded into a computer navigation system to plan the optimal entry trajectory from the scalp surface into the lateral and third ventricles.
  • Anatomy & Basilar Artery Assessment: Careful imaging review of the basilar artery and its branches, which lie directly below the third ventricle floor, to ensure a safe, precise puncture site.
  • Pediatric & Adult Readiness Evaluation: Assessing age-specific suitability (calculating the ETV Success Score), as older infants and adults generally exhibit higher success rates than newborn infants.

What Happens During the Surgery?

ETV is performed through a keyhole approach using advanced neuroendoscopic equipment and real-time guidance:

  • Stage 1: Anesthesia & Keyhole Access: Under general anesthesia, a small incision (hidden behind the hairline) and a single tiny skull opening (burr hole) are created over the right frontal region.
  • Stage 2: Endoscopic Ventricular Entry: A delicate neuroendoscope equipped with a camera, illumination light, and working channels is gently guided into the right lateral ventricle and through the Foramen of Monro into the third ventricle.
  • Stage 3: Inspection of the Third Ventricle Floor: The surgeon inspects the thin membrane forming the floor of the third ventricle (tuber cinereum) and identifies key anatomical landmarks, including the mammillary bodies and basilar artery below.
  • Stage 4: Ventriculostomy Creation & Balloon Dilation: A tiny hole is made in the floor of the third ventricle using a micro-forceps or neurovascular probe. A small inflatable catheter balloon is inserted into the opening and gently expanded to dilate the bypass hole to a safe size.
  • Stage 5: Verification of Flow & Closure: Direct visual inspection confirms CSF pulsating freely through the opening into the basal cisterns. The endoscope is withdrawn, the burr hole is sealed, and the scalp incision is closed with cosmetic sutures.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery following an ETV is typically swift due to the minimally invasive keyhole approach, with close monitoring focused on verifying long-term fluid drainage:

  • ICU / High-Dependency Monitoring (First 24 Hours): Patients are monitored for neurological recovery, resolution of pre-operative headaches, and overall stability in an ICU or specialized neuro-ward.
  • Early Hospital Discharge: Because no permanent shunt hardware is implanted, patients often experience short hospital stays (typically 1 to 3 days) once normal eating and walking resume.
  • Wound & Incision Care: The tiny frontal scalp incision is kept clean and dry, with sutures or clips removed around 7 to 10 days post-operatively.
  • Follow-Up Imaging & Stoma Monitoring: A follow-up MRI (CINE CSF flow study) is performed around 6 to 12 weeks after surgery to confirm that the ventriculostomy hole (stoma) remains widely open and fluid pressure remains well-balanced.
