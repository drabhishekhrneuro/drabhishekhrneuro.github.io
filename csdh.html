Chronic Subdural Hematoma

What Is a Chronic Subdural Hematoma?

Beneath your hard skull bone and the tough outer membrane wrapping your brain (the dura), tiny, delicate veins carry blood away from the brain's surface.

A chronic subdural hematoma occurs when these small veins slowly leak blood into the space underneath the dura, forming a collection of blood that gradually expands over several weeks or even months.

Unlike an acute head injury that causes immediate, severe symptoms, a chronic subdural hematoma grows very slowly. It often starts from a minor, easily forgotten bump to the head—especially in older adults or individuals taking blood-thinning medications. Because the blood accumulates gradually, the brain slowly adapts to the squeezing pressure at first, which is why symptoms can take weeks to noticeably appear.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because pressure builds up slowly over time, symptoms often mimic general aging, fatigue, or early memory loss, making them easy to overlook initially:

Gradual Memory & Confusion Shifts

Subtle changes in thinking, increased forgetfulness, disorientation, or difficulty following conversations.

Persistent, Dull Headaches

An ongoing, low-grade headache that slowly worsens over time rather than arriving suddenly.

Balance & Walking Difficulties

Unsteadiness on the feet, frequent tripping, or a slow, shuffling gait.

One-Sided Weakness or Clumsiness

Mild weakness, numbness, or loss of coordination in one arm or leg, sometimes causing people to drop objects.

Speech Difficulties

Slurred speech or mild trouble finding the right words.

Increasing Drowsiness

Sleeping significantly more than usual or having trouble staying fully alert throughout the day.

Common Risk Factors

Several factors make the delicate veins on the surface of the brain more prone to slow leaking:

  • Minor Head Trauma: Even a soft bump on a cabinet door or a mild sudden stop in a vehicle can stretch and tear fragile veins.
  • Older Age: As the brain naturally shrinks slightly with age, the space beneath the dura widens, putting extra tension on surface veins.
  • Blood-Thinning Medications: Blood thinners or antiplatelet drugs make it harder for small vascular leaks to clot quickly on their own.
  • Frequent Falls: Conditions that affect balance or mobility increase the chance of recurring minor head impacts.

How Is It Discovered?

Because symptoms develop gradually, clear brain imaging is essential to distinguish a chronic hematoma from other neurological conditions:

  • Brain CT Scan: The primary diagnostic test. A CT scan quickly reveals the old blood collection as a dark, crescent-shaped area pressing against the surface of the brain.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Provides highly detailed images to assess the age of the blood collection and check for multiple fluid layers or internal membranes.
  • Neurological Examination: A thorough check of mental alertness, muscle strength, reflexes, and balance to evaluate how much pressure the brain is experiencing.

How Is It Treated?

Treatment depends on the size of the blood collection and the severity of the symptoms:

  • Observation & Medication: Small hematomas that cause minimal or no symptoms can often be monitored closely with repeated CT scans. Medications may be used to reduce localized inflammation or temporarily manage blood-thinning treatments.
  • Burr Hole Drainage: A gentle, highly effective, and minimally invasive surgical procedure. A neurosurgeon makes one or two tiny openings (burr holes) in the skull, carefully washes out the old fluid collection, and places a temporary drainage tube for a day or two to relieve pressure entirely.
  • Craniotomy: In rare cases where the fluid has formed thick internal membranes or firm blood clots, a slightly larger temporary opening in the bone may be made to ensure thorough removal.
  • Middle Meningeal Artery (MMA) Embolization: An advanced, minimally invasive endovascular procedure where a doctor passes a tiny catheter through the blood vessels to seal off the tiny arterial branches feeding the hematoma membrane, stopping fluid re-accumulation and preventing recurrence.
  • Recovery & Relief: Because the underlying brain tissue is usually uninjured, relieving the pressure often leads to rapid and significant improvements in alertness, walking, and cognitive clarity.
