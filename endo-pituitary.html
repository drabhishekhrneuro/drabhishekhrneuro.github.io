Endoscopic Endonasal Pituitary Surgery

What Is Endoscopic Endonasal Pituitary Surgery?

Imagine the pituitary gland—the body’s master hormone controller—nestled safely at the very center of the base of your skull, sitting directly behind your nasal passages. Traditionally, reaching this delicate gland required an incision through the skull or under the upper lip. Today, modern neurosurgery allows us to reach this deep target through a completely natural corridor: the nose.

Endoscopic endonasal pituitary surgery is a minimally invasive technique where a high-definition endoscope (a slender camera) and specialized micro-instruments are guided through the nasal cavity to remove pituitary tumours without making any external facial or scalp incisions.

Think of this procedure as a high-precision, keyhole corridor to the base of the brain:

  • Natural Pathway (No External Cuts): By utilizing the nasal passages, the neurosurgeon avoids cutting skin, muscle, or skull bone, resulting in less post-operative pain and a faster overall recovery.
  • Panoramic Visualization: High-definition endoscopes provide bright, wide-angle, and close-up views around corners inside the skull base, helping protect crucial surrounding structures like the optic nerves and carotid arteries.
  • Surgical Goals: The objectives are to completely remove hormone-secreting or vision-threatening pituitary tumours (adenomas), decompress the optic chiasm to restore normal vision, and preserve healthy pituitary gland function.

When Is This Surgery Recommended?

This procedure is indicated for pituitary adenomas and related skull base lesions that cause hormonal imbalances or press against neighboring visual and neural pathways:

Vision Loss & Chiasmal Compression

When a growing pituitary tumour presses upward against the optic chiasm, causing progressive loss of side (peripheral) vision or blurred sight.

Hormone-Secreting Tumours

Indicated for active tumours producing excess hormones, such as prolactinomas unresponsive to medication, growth hormone-secreting tumours causing acromegaly, or ACTH-secreting tumours causing Cushing's disease.

Pituitary Apoplexy (Emergency)

When sudden bleeding or infarction inside a pituitary tumour causes acute, severe headaches, rapid vision loss, or altered consciousness requiring urgent decompression.

Large Non-Functioning Adenomas

Masses that grow large enough to crowd out normal pituitary function (hypopituitarism) or cause persistent, severe pressure headaches.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Comprehensive preoperative evaluation ensures accurate anatomical mapping and endocrine preparation before entering the skull base:

  • High-Resolution Pituitary MRI: Detailed 3D thin-slice MRI scans with contrast to map the exact dimensions of the tumour, its relationship to the carotid arteries, and extension toward the optic nerves.
  • Formal Visual Field Testing: Automated perimetry testing conducted by an ophthalmologist to document baseline peripheral vision deficits and track recovery after surgery.
  • Comprehensive Endocrine Panel: Blood work evaluating baseline hormone levels across the entire pituitary axis (thyroid, adrenal, gonadal, and growth axes) to guide post-operative hormone replacement planning.
  • Nasal Evaluation & CT Sinus Scan: Imaging and clinical assessment of nasal anatomy to plan the safest transnasal corridor and coordinate with ear, nose, and throat (ENT) colleagues if a combined approach is utilized.

What Happens During the Surgery?

The procedure is performed collaboratively or under specialized neurosurgical technique in structured phases:

  • Stage 1: Anesthesia & Nasal Preparation: Under general anesthesia, the nasal passages are prepared with decongestant solutions to minimize bleeding and optimize working space.
  • Stage 2: Endoscopic Nasal Approach: Guided by the high-definition endoscope, the surgeon navigates through the nasal cavity, gently moving or trimming the nasal turbinates to reach the sphenoid sinus cavity at the back of the nose.
  • Stage 3: Opening the Sella Floor: Using micro-drills and delicate instruments, a small opening is created through the thin bone of the sella turcica (the bony pocket housing the pituitary gland) to expose the protective dura membrane.
  • Stage 4: Microsurgical Tumour Removal: The dura is opened, and specialized micro-instruments are used to meticulously dissect and remove the soft pituitary adenoma tissue while preserving the normal gland and monitoring nearby nerves.
  • Stage 5: Skull Base Reconstruction & Closure: To prevent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks, the bony defect is carefully sealed using a small biological graft (such as a nasal mucosal flap or fat graft) secured with surgical glue. Nasal splints or soft packing may be placed temporarily.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery following endoscopic pituitary surgery focuses on comfortable nasal healing, hormone monitoring, and safeguarding restored vision:

  • Post-Op Monitoring (First 24–48 Hours): Patients recover in the neuro-surgical unit or ICU, where medical staff closely monitor fluid balance, electrolyte levels (checking for diabetes insipidus), vision, and alertness.
  • Nasal Healing & Care: Mild nasal congestion, crusting, or minor blood-tinged discharge is normal for the first few weeks. Saline nasal rinses are prescribed to keep passages clean. Blowing the nose, heavy lifting, and bending over are strictly restricted for several weeks to protect the skull base repair.
  • Endocrine & Hormone Tracking: Blood hormone levels are checked frequently during recovery. Temporary or permanent hormone replacement medications (such as hydrocortisone, thyroid hormone, or desmopressin) may be prescribed based on pituitary function.
  • Follow-Up Imaging & Visual Testing: Follow-up visual field examinations and a post-operative pituitary MRI are typically scheduled within 3 months to confirm complete tumour removal and assess healing.
