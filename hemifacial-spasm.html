Hemifacial Spasm

What Is Hemifacial Spasm?

Imagine the facial nerve (the seventh cranial nerve) as a main power trunk cable that carries motion commands from your brainstem to the tiny muscles controlling your facial expressions—allowing you to blink, smile, frown, and raise your eyebrows seamlessly.

Hemifacial spasm is a neuromuscular disorder characterized by frequent, involuntary, painless twitching or contractions of the muscles on one side (hemi-) of the face.

Think of the facial nerve as a control wire under constant mechanical irritation:

  • Vascular Compression (Neurovascular Conflict): In almost all cases, an adjacent blood vessel (typically a loop of a normal pulsating artery) presses directly against the facial nerve right where it exits the brainstem (root exit zone).
  • Worn Nerve Insulation: Continuous vascular pulsation against the nerve causes gradual wear on its protective myelin sheath (demyelination), creating an irritable site along the nerve pathway.
  • Cross-Talk (Ephaptic Transmission): Electrical signals jumping across adjacent damaged nerve fibers cause unprompted, chaotic muscle contractions that flutter down one side of the face independently of your conscious control.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Hemifacial spasm typically follows a distinct pattern, starting subtle and gradually spreading across one side of the face:

Involuntary Eye Twitching (Blepharospasm)

Intermittent, subtle twitching or fluttering starting around the eyelid (orbicularis oculi muscle), often mistaken for stress-induced eye twitches in early stages.

Downward Facial Spread

Over months or years, spasms gradually spread down the same side of the face to involve the cheek, mouth corner, lip, chin, and neck muscles (platysma).

Sustained Muscle Contractions

Brief, rapid flutters progress to longer, sustained muscle spasms that can forcefully pull the corner of the mouth upward or pinch the eye shut tightly.

Stress & Fatigue Triggers

Spasms are exacerbated by stress, fatigue, anxiety, bright lights, or intentional facial movements like smiling, chewing, or talking.

Auditory Clicking Sound

A mild clicking or thumping sound in the ear on the affected side occurring during spasms, caused by contractions of a tiny muscle inside the ear (stapedius muscle).

How is it Discovered?

Diagnosis relies heavily on clinical expert examination combined with specialized, high-resolution neuroimaging to map the nerve-vessel junction:

  • High-Resolution 3T Brain MRI (FIESTA / CISS Sequences): Thin-slice, high-contrast MRI sequences specifically designed to visualize the facial nerve root exit zone and clearly identify compressing arterial loops at the brainstem.
  • Brain MRI with Contrast: Diagnostic imaging performed to rule out secondary causes of facial twitching, such as cerebellopontine angle (CPA) tumours, meningiomas, epidermoid cysts, or vascular malformations.
  • Electromyography (EMG) & Lateral Spread Response: Specialized electrophysiological testing demonstrating the unique "lateral spread response"—a hallmark electrical pattern where stimulating one branch of the facial nerve abnormally triggers muscle contraction across other branches.

How is it Treated?

Treatment ranges from temporary symptom suppression to high-precision surgical decompression that provides a permanent cure:

  • Botulinum Toxin Injections: Targeted, precise injections into affected facial muscles to temporarily block neuromuscular signals, relaxing spasms and providing symptom relief for 3 to 4 months before repeat treatment is required.
  • Microvascular Decompression (MVD / Jannetta Procedure): The primary curative neurosurgical treatment. Through a small keyhole opening behind the ear (retrosigmoid approach), the surgeon uses an operating microscope to separate the pulsating artery from the facial nerve root and place a soft, inert Teflon cushion between them to permanently relieve mechanical compression.
  • Oral Neuropathic Medications: Prescription muscle relaxants or anticonvulsants (such as Baclofen or Clonazepam) evaluated in early or mild cases, though they generally provide limited long-term control compared to botulinum toxin or MVD.
  • Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM): Continuous real-time monitoring of facial nerve signals and lateral spread response during MVD surgery to verify complete nerve decompression and maximize patient safety.
