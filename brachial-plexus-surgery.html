Peripheral Nerve Surgery

Brachial Plexus Surgery & Nerve Repair

What Is Brachial Plexus Repair?

Imagine your neck and arm connected by a major electrical distribution network: the brachial plexus. This complex network of nerve roots originates from the cervical spinal cord (C5 to T1 levels), merging and branching to control all muscle movement and sensation across the shoulder, arm, forearm, and hand. When high-energy trauma (such as motorcycle accidents, heavy falls, or birth injury) stretches, tears, or severs these nerve cables, signal transmission between the brain and arm is disrupted.

Brachial Plexus Surgery encompasses specialized peripheral nerve operations designed to reconstruct the damaged nerve network. Utilizing micro-sutures, high-power operating microscopes, and advanced nerve grafting or nerve transfer techniques, neurosurgeons bypass destroyed nerve segments, clear restrictive scar tissue (neurolysis), and restore electrical connectivity to re-innervate paralyzed limb muscles.

Think of brachial plexus repair as rewiring a severed high-voltage power grid to restore movement and sensation to the arm:

  • Restores Signal Delivery: Re-establishes physical continuity across severed or severely scarred nerve channels before target muscle endplates permanently atrophy.
  • Surgical Technique Selection: Operative strategies range from direct primary micro-stitching and donor nerve autografts to innovative nerve transfer procedures (borrowing functional redundant nerves from nearby healthy muscles).
  • Time-Sensitive Intervention: Because nerve fibers regrow slowly (approximately 1 millimeter per day), timely surgical reconstruction within a critical post-injury window is vital to preserve muscle function.

When Is Brachial Plexus Surgery Recommended?

Surgery is indicated when traumatic injury or compressive lesions cause significant neurological deficits that fail to recover spontaneously:

Traumatic Nerve Avulsion or Laceration

Complete tearing or root detachment of nerve cables caused by severe traction impacts, leading to total motor paralysis and numbness across the shoulder, arm, or hand.

Flail Arm & Severe Limb Weakness

Inability to lift the shoulder, flex the elbow, or use the wrist and hand following high-energy trauma (such as high-speed vehicular or sports injuries).

Lack of Spontaneous Recovery on Electrodiagnostics

Serial EMG and nerve conduction studies (evaluated over 3 to 6 months post-injury) showing no electrical signs of nerve re-innervation or functional muscle recovery.

Obstetric Brachial Plexus Palsy (Erb's Palsy)

Infants with birth-related traction injuries who show persistent elbow, shoulder, or wrist weakness that fails to improve within the first 3 to 6 months of life.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Comprehensive neuro-diagnostic mapping and functional testing dictate surgical trajectory and donor vessel/nerve selection:

  • High-Resolution Brachial Plexus MRI (with Neurography Sequences): Maps nerve continuity, identifies pseudomeningoceles (indicating nerve root avulsion from the spinal cord), and assesses soft-tissue scarring.
  • Electrodiagnostic Studies (EMG & NCV): Evaluates muscle innervation, distinguishes partial nerve stretch (neuropraxia) from complete axon disruption (axonotmesis/neurotmesis), and identifies viable donor nerves.
  • Detailed Physical & Functional Motor Grading: Baseline muscle strength testing (MRC scale 0–5) across individual nerve distributions to target critical functional priorities (such as elbow flexion and shoulder abduction).
  • Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM) Setup: Preparing real-time nerve action potential (NAP) and somatosensory (SSEP) recording to evaluate nerve signal conduction across damaged segments during surgery.

What Happens During the Surgery?

The complex operation is performed under general anesthesia utilizing microsurgical standards and continuous electrical nerve testing:

  • Stage 1: Position & Surgical Exposure: The patient is positioned comfortably on their back with the neck, supraclavicular area, chest, and arm prepared. An incision is made above or below the collarbone (supraclavicular/infraclavicular approach) to expose the plexus.
  • Stage 2: Microsurgical Exploration & Neurolysis: Working under high-magnification operating optics, the surgeon carefully frees the plexus from scar tissue and performs intraoperative electrical stimulation (NAPs) to test nerve conduction across damaged segments.
  • Stage 3: Nerve Reconstruction Strategy:
    • Direct Micro-Suture Repair: Sharp, clean nerve cuts are directly sutured end-to-end using micro-sutures finer than a human hair.
    • Nerve Autografting: When a gap exists between nerve ends, healthy donor nerve segments (typically the sural nerve taken from the leg) are bridged across the gap to guide regenerating nerve fibers.
    • Nerve Transfer (Neurotization): If a nerve root is completely torn from the spinal cord, a functional redundant nerve branch (such as intercostal nerves, spinal accessory nerve, or Oberlin transfer branches) is re-routed and connected directly to the paralyzed target nerve close to the muscle.
  • Stage 4: Intraoperative Verification & Graft Fixation: Coaptation sites are secured using ultra-fine sutures and biological fibrin glue to ensure tension-free nerve junctions.
  • Stage 5: Multi-Layer Closure & Splinting: Incisions are closed meticulously in multiple layers over surgical drains, and the neck and arm are supported in a protective brace to prevent tension on the delicate nerve repairs.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery is a gradual process requiring limb protection, dedicated physical therapy, and long-term monitoring:

  • Immobilization Phase (3 to 4 Weeks): The shoulder and neck are supported in a specialized brace or sling to prevent tension across the re-connected nerve grafts or transfers while early healing occurs.
  • Incision Care & Outpatient Discharge: Hospital stay typically lasts 2 to 4 days. Drains are removed, and wound care instructions are provided prior to discharge.
  • Targeted Physical & Occupational Therapy: Guided physical therapy, joint range-of-motion exercises, and electrical muscle stimulation begin early to keep joints flexible and preserve muscle tone while nerve fibers slowly regenerate.
  • Long-Term Bio-Feedback & Functional Re-Education: Nerve fibers regenerate at roughly 1 mm per day. Functional recovery unfolds over 6 to 24 months, with specialized motor re-education helping the brain learn to control arm movements through newly transferred nerve pathways.
