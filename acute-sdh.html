Head Injury

Acute Subdural Hematoma

What Is an Acute Subdural Hematoma?

To understand a subdural hematoma, it helps to look at how blood drains from the brain. Beneath the hard skull and the tough outer membrane (the dura), tiny, delicate veins act like bridges, carrying blood away from the surface of the brain back toward the heart.

An acute subdural hematoma occurs when a sudden, violent movement or severe blow to the head tears these fragile "bridging veins." Blood leaks out and collects in the space underneath the dura, spreading directly over the surface of the brain like a rapidly expanding sheet.

Because the skull cannot expand, this accumulating pool of blood presses directly against sensitive brain tissue. Unlike an extradural hematoma (which sits outside the dura and forms a compact, lens-shaped pocket), an acute subdural hematoma often spreads widely over a large section of the brain surface and frequently accompanies direct bruising to the brain tissue underneath.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms usually appear rapidly within hours of a head impact—such as from a car collision, a severe fall, or a high-impact sporting injury:

Immediate or Progressive Unconsciousness

Passing out right at the time of injury or becoming rapidly drowsy, unresponsive or difficult to wake up.

Severe, Worsening Headache

An intense, constant headache driven by rising pressure inside the skull.

Confusion & Speech Changes

Slurred speech, disorientation, extreme agitation or inability to follow simple commands.

Weakness on One Side of the Body

Difficulty moving an arm or leg on the opposite side of the head injury.

Pupil Changes

One pupil becoming enlarged (dilated) or failing to react to light.

Seizures

Involuntary twitching or convulsions caused by pressure and irritation on the brain’s outer surface.

Emergency Warning: An acute subdural hematoma is a life-threatening medical emergency. Any significant head injury followed by loss of consciousness, confusion or weakness requires immediate emergency medical treatment.

How Is It Discovered?

Rapid imaging is essential to evaluate the injury and guide immediate treatment:

  • Emergency Brain CT Scan: A CT scan is the fastest and most reliable test. It clearly reveals an acute subdural hematoma as a crescent-shaped band of blood curving along the inner contour of the skull and pressing against the brain.
  • Neurological Evaluation: Doctors continuously assess the patient's responsiveness, pupil reflexes and movement patterns to monitor changes in brain pressure.

How Is It Treated?

Treatment focuses on removing the pressure as quickly as possible and protecting the brain:

  • Emergency Surgery (Craniotomy or Craniectomy): For large blood collections or when brain pressure is dangerously high, a neurosurgeon removes a section of the skull bone to gently evacuate the blood clot and relieve pressure. If the brain is severely swollen, the surgeon may temporarily leave the bone flap off (a decompressive craniectomy) to give the brain room to swell safely without being compressed.
  • Intensive Care Unit (ICU) Management: Specialized care in an ICU is crucial. Medications are used to control brain swelling, regulate blood pressure, prevent seizures and support breathing.
  • Rehabilitation: Because the underlying brain tissue may have experienced direct impact, recovery often involves specialized physical, occupational and speech therapy to help the patient regain strength, mobility and cognitive functions.
