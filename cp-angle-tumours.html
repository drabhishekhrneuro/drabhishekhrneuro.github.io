Cerebellopontine Angle (CPA) Tumours

What Is a Cerebellopontine Angle (CPA) Tumour?

Imagine your brain's posterior region as a busy junction where the cerebellum (your balance coordinator) sits adjacent to the pons (a vital part of the brainstem managing vital functions and facial movement). The specialized anatomical space formed between these two structures is called the cerebellopontine angle (CPA).

A CPA tumour is a growth—most commonly non-cancerous (benign)—that develops within this small, delicate anatomical junction at the base of the skull.

Think of the CPA space as a tight electrical conduit filled with critical nerves and blood vessels:

  • Nerve & Brainstem Proximity: Key cranial nerves controlling hearing, balance, facial movement, facial sensation, and swallowing pass directly through or adjacent to this small triangular space.
  • Common Types: The vast majority of CPA tumors are benign, with vestibular schwannomas (acoustic neuromas, arising from the balance nerve sheath) accounting for nearly 80-85%, followed by meningiomas (arising from the brain lining) and epidermoid cysts.
  • Gradual Crowding: Because the CPA is a fixed bony space at the skull base, as the tumour slowly enlarges, it compresses neighboring cranial nerves, pushes against the cerebellum, and can eventually press upon the brainstem.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because CPA tumours typically grow slowly, symptoms develop gradually over months or years, initially affecting hearing and balance before impacting facial nerves:

Unilateral Hearing Loss

Progressive or sudden decline in hearing in one ear, often noticed when holding a telephone to that ear or in noisy environments.

Tinnitus (Ringing in One Ear)

A constant or intermittent high-pitched ringing, buzzing, or roaring sound isolated to the affected ear.

Unsteadiness & Dizziness

A persistent feeling of imbalance, lightheadedness, or brief episodes of vertigo (spinning sensations) caused by pressure on the balance nerve.

Facial Numbness, Tingling, or Weakness

"Pins and needles" or reduced sensation on one side of the face, or subtle weakness/twitching around the eye or mouth due to trigeminal or facial nerve compression.

Headaches & Walking Instability (Large Tumours)

Persistent headaches, clumsiness, or difficulty walking in a straight line as larger tumours exert pressure on the cerebellum, brainstem, or spinal fluid pathways.

How is it Discovered?

Early diagnosis requires combining high-resolution MRI with specialized nerve and audiologic testing:

  • Dedicated Contrast-Enhanced Brain MRI: The absolute gold-standard diagnostic tool. Thin-slice MRI focusing on the internal auditory canal and posterior fossa clearly visualizes the tumour's exact size, origin, and relationship to the brainstem and cranial nerves.
  • Audiometry & Speech Discrimination Testing: Formal hearing tests conducted by an audiologist to quantify tone thresholds and speech comprehension in each ear.
  • Brainstem Auditory Evoked Response (BAER): A diagnostic test measuring electrical signal speed along the hearing nerve pathway from the ear to the brainstem.
  • High-Resolution Temporal Bone CT Scan: Detailed bony imaging used to evaluate the internal auditory canal, skull base anatomy, and surgical landmarks prior to intervention.

How is it Treated?

Treatment strategy depends on tumour size, growth rate, age, hearing status, and overall symptoms, utilizing three primary approaches:

  • Observation & Serial MRI Surveillance ("Wait and Scan"): For small, slow-growing, or incidentally discovered tumours in patients with stable symptoms or minimal hearing loss, periodic clinical exams and annual MRI scans are performed.
  • Microsurgical Resection (Retrosigmoid / Translabyrinthine / Middle Fossa): High-precision skull base neurosurgery performed under an operating microscope with real-time facial and acoustic nerve monitoring to remove the tumour while protecting brainstem and facial nerve function.
  • Stereotactic Radiosurgery (Gamma Knife / CyberKnife): Advanced, single-session or hypofractionated precision radiation focused directly on the tumour to halt its growth, preserving existing facial nerve function and serving as an effective alternative to surgery for small-to-medium tumours.
  • Targeted Rehabilitation: Vestibular physical therapy to retrain balance, audiologic support (hearing aids or implantable devices), and specialized facial nerve rehabilitation when needed post-treatment.
