Brachial Plexus Injury

What Is a Brachial Plexus Injury?

A brachial plexus injury happens when the network of nerves connecting your spinal cord to your shoulder, arm and hand gets stretched, compressed or torn.

This nerve network acts as the main "electrical control center" for your upper body. It tells your shoulder, elbow, wrist and fingers how to move and sends feeling back up to your brain.

Think of the brachial plexus as a main power cable branching out from your neck down into your arm:

  • The Sudden Pull: An unexpected force pushes your head one way while pulling your shoulder down in the opposite direction (like a hard impact during sports or a motorcycle fall).
  • The Stretch or Tear: This forceful separation stretches the "wiring." Mild cases overstretch the nerve fibers, while severe impacts can tear the nerves partially or pull them completely away from the spinal cord root.
  • The Communication Cut: Depending on how stretched the wires are, signals traveling to your arm get fuzzy, delayed or cut off entirely.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms vary depending on whether the nerves are just pinched/stretched or torn:

"Burners" or "Stingers"

A sharp, burning shock or warm electric sensation shooting down your shoulder and arm after a hard tackle or collision, usually lasting a few seconds to a few minutes.

Arm Numbness or Weakness

Feeling like your arm has "fallen asleep" or is temporarily too heavy to lift, making it tough to brush your hair or grip a cup.

Inability to Use Specific Muscles

Difficulty bending your elbow, raising your arm above your shoulder or flexing your wrist and fingers.

Severe, Constant Pain

In severe nerve tears, people may experience a burning, crushing pain in the arm despite having little to no movement or feeling in it.

Common Triggers

Brachial plexus injuries are typically caused by traumatic stretching, high-impact force, or birth-related complications:

  • Contact Sports: High-impact collisions in football, rugby or wrestling that force the neck and shoulder apart.
  • Motorcycle or Auto Accidents: High-speed impacts where the shoulder strikes the ground or vehicle frame with significant force.
  • Difficult Births (Erb's Palsy): Newborns can experience nerve stretching during a difficult delivery if their shoulders get caught in the birth canal.
  • Falls or Heavy Trauma: Falling from a height and landing awkwardly on the shoulder or side of the neck.

How Is It Treated & Managed?

Recovery depends on the severity of the stretch or tear, ranging from conservative physical therapy to advanced micro-neurosurgical procedures:

  • Observation for Mild Injuries: Many "stingers" and minor stretches heal on their own with short-term rest, ice and physical therapy to preserve joint range of motion.
  • Targeted Rehabilitation: Exercises to prevent muscle atrophy (wasting away) and keep joints flexible while the nerve slowly regenerates (nerves heal very slowly—roughly 1 millimeter per day).
  • Surgical Evaluation: Surgery is considered if a nerve is completely torn or pulled away from the spinal cord, or if there is no recovery seen within 3 to 6 months of non-surgical care.
  • Surgical Reconstruction Options: Surgeons can perform nerve grafts (taking a healthy nerve from another part of the body to bridge the gap) or nerve transfers (rerouting a nearby working nerve to power the affected arm muscle).
  • Importance of Surgical Timing: For severe injuries, surgical evaluation is usually recommended within 3 to 6 months of the injury to get the best possible recovery results before muscle tissue weakens permanently.
