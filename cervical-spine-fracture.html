Cervical Spine Fracture (Broken Neck)

What Is a Cervical Spine Fracture?

Your neck is made up of seven stacked bones called the cervical vertebrae (labeled C1 through C7). Think of them like seven hollow wooden blocks stacked neatly on top of one another. Passing straight down through the hollow middle of these blocks is your spinal cord—the main electrical cable that carries commands from your brain to the rest of your body.

A cervical spine fracture—commonly called a broken neck—happens when a strong impact cracks, breaks or crushes one or more of these neck bones.

Imagine a protective casing surrounding a delicate wire. A crack in the casing is serious, but if the broken bone shifts out of place, it can squeeze or pinch the wire inside. That is why treating a broken neck is always about two things: fixing the bone break and keeping the spinal cord completely safe.

How Do They Happen?

Neck fractures usually happen when a sudden force twists, snaps or slams the head and neck beyond their normal limits. Common real-world examples include:

  • Car & Motorcycle Accidents: Sudden stopping or impact that violently jerks the head forward, backward or sideways (extreme whiplash).
  • High Falls: Falling off a ladder, roof or slipping on stairs and landing hard on the upper back, neck or head.
  • Sports & Recreational Injuries: Diving headfirst into shallow water, hard collisions in contact sports (like rugby or football), or tumbling off a bicycle or horse.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

The symptoms of a broken neck depend heavily on whether the broken bone stayed in its normal position or moved enough to touch the spinal cord:

Immediate, Severe Neck Pain

Intense pain right along the back of the neck immediately following an impact.

Neck Stiffness & Muscle Spasms

The neck muscles go into a tight, protective spasm, making it almost impossible or extremely painful to turn or tilt the head.

Numbness, Tingling, or "Electric Shocks"

"Pins and needles" or an electric sensation shooting down the shoulders, arms or fingers—a clear sign that a nerve root is being irritated.

Weakness in the Arms or Legs

Finding it hard to grip objects, lift your arms or move your legs normally.

Trouble Breathing or Loss of Sensation (Severe Cases)

If the injury occurs high up near the top of the neck and presses heavily on the spinal cord, it can disrupt breathing muscles or cause loss of feeling below the neck.

Emergency First Aid Tip: If you suspect someone has a neck injury, do not move them. Keep their head and neck completely still until emergency responders arrive. Moving a broken neck bone can cause a stable break to shift and pinch the spinal cord.

How Is It Discovered?

Emergency teams use clear, quick imaging to look at the bones and check the spinal cord:

  • CT Scan of the Neck: The fastest and most accurate test for bone breaks. A CT scan takes detailed, 3D cross-section pictures of all seven neck bones, showing every crack or displaced piece within seconds.
  • MRI Scan: While a CT scan shows the bones clearly, an MRI acts like an soft-tissue camera. It checks if the spinal cord is pinched, bruised or swollen and whether any supporting neck ligaments are torn.
  • X-Rays: Used in emergency triage to quickly spot major bone alignment shifts.

How Is It Treated?

Treatment depends on whether the fracture is stable (the bone pieces stay neatly in place) or unstable (the bone has shifted or risks moving and hurting the spinal cord):

  • Rigid Neck Collar (Bracing): For clean, stable cracks where the bones haven't moved, wearing a firm neck collar (like a rigid plastic Philadelphia or Aspen brace) for 6 to 12 weeks holds the neck still so the bone can heal naturally, much like a cast on a broken arm.
  • Halo Brace (In Select Cases): A specialized ring brace anchored around the head and attached to a light vest worn on the chest. It keeps the head completely still without surgery while complex bones heal.
  • Surgical Realignment & Fusion: If the broken bone has shifted, fragmented or threatens the spinal cord, a neurosurgeon performs surgery. The surgeon gently realigns the bone pieces, removes any fragments pressing on nerves and uses small medical-grade titanium plates, rods or screws to lock the bones firmly in place so they can fuse into solid bone.
