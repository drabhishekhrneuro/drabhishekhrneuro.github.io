Spinal Abscess

What Is a Spinal Abscess?

A spinal abscess is a swollen, fluid-filled pocket of infection that forms around or inside your spinal cord.

Most commonly, it occurs in the space between the bones of your spine and the outer protective lining of the spinal cord—known as a spinal epidural abscess. It is a serious medical emergency because as pus builds up inside the rigid bony spinal canal, it squeezes the spinal cord and nerve roots, cutting off critical signals and blood flow.

Think of your spine as a protective conduit carrying main electrical cables:

  • The Intruder: Bacteria (often Staphylococcus) enter the spine region, frequently traveling through the bloodstream from an infection somewhere else in the body or entering directly after a spinal procedure or trauma.
  • The Walled-Off Collection: Your immune system sends white blood cells to trap the bacteria, forming a pocket of pus and inflamed tissue inside the spinal canal.
  • The Pinch Point: Because the spine is enclosed in hard bone, the growing pocket has nowhere to expand. It presses directly against the spinal cord and branching nerves, acting like a heavy weight crushing sensitive electrical wires.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms often follow a progressive pattern over a few days or weeks as pressure on the spinal cord builds:

Severe, Localized Back Pain

A deep, intense ache in a specific spot on your spine that feels very tender when pressed or tapped.

Fever and Chills

High body temperature, night sweats and feeling generally sick as your body fights the infection.

Shooting Nerve Pain (Radiculopathy)

Sharp, electric-like pain radiating down from your back into your legs or arms, depending on where the abscess is located.

Numbness or Weakness

Feeling like your legs are unusually heavy, wobbly or "asleep," making it difficult to climb stairs or walk steadily.

Red-Flag Warning: Sudden loss of bowel or bladder control (incontinence or inability to urinate) or severe, rapidly worsening leg weakness signals critical spinal cord compression requiring immediate emergency care.

How Infection Gets There

Bacteria typically gain entry to the spinal canal through three main pathways:

  • Bloodstream Travel: Bacteria traveling from skin infections, dental infections, urinary tract infections or intravenous (IV) drug use.
  • Spreading Nearby: Infection spreading directly from nearby infected spinal bones (osteomyelitis) or disc spaces (discitis).
  • Direct Access: Rare complications following spinal surgery, epidural injections or severe penetrating back trauma.

Emergency Care & Treatment

Managing a spinal abscess requires immediate, specialized hospital care, urgent imaging, and rapid decompression:

  • Intravenous (IV) Antibiotics: High-dose antibiotics targeted at the specific bacteria, given through an IV line for several weeks.
  • Emergency Scans: An urgent MRI scan to pinpoint the exact location and size of the abscess.
  • Surgical Indications: Because a growing abscess can cause permanent nerve damage or paralysis within hours to days, surgery is frequently required right away as soon as scans confirm spinal cord compression or progressive weakness.
  • Surgical Decompression (Laminectomy & Drainage): A spine surgeon creates a small opening in the back of the spinal bone (laminectomy), drains the pus collection, flushes the area, and places temporary drains to instantly relieve pressure on the spinal cord.
