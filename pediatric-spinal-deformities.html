Pediatric Spinal Deformities

What Is Pediatric Spinal Deformity?

A healthy spine, when viewed from behind, runs straight down the middle of the back like a balanced central pillar. When viewed from the side, it has gentle, natural curves that act like a shock absorber when walking, running or jumping.

Pediatric spinal deformities occur when a child's growing spine develops abnormal curves, twists, or bends. Instead of growing straight and aligned, the spine may curve sideways, hunch forward excessively, or rotate out of position.

Because a child’s body is growing rapidly, catching and addressing these curves early prevents them from causing back fatigue, posture issues, or in severe cases, restricting space for the heart and lungs.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

In the early stages, spinal deformities are rarely painful. Parents or school health screenings often notice subtle changes in appearance first:

Uneven Shoulders or Waist

One shoulder appears higher than the other, or the waistline looks uneven.

Prominent Shoulder Blade

One shoulder blade sticks out more noticeably than the other, especially when bending forward.

Leaning to One Side

The head doesn't look centered directly over the pelvis, or the body tilts slightly to one side.

Postural Fatigue

A dull ache or tiring feeling in the back after standing or sitting for long periods.

Clothing Misalignment

Noticing that shirt necklines slip to one side or pant legs hang unevenly.

How Is It Discovered?

Evaluating a child's spine requires a careful look at their growth trajectory and bone maturity:

  • Adam's Forward Bend Test: A quick, simple physical test where the child bends forward at the waist. This allows the doctor to see any rotational rib hump or spinal asymmetry clearly.
  • Full-Length Standing X-Rays: Specialized X-rays taken while standing show the entire spine from neck to pelvis. Doctors use these images to measure the exact degree of the curve.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): An MRI scan is performed if an underlying spinal cord issue (such as a tethered cord or syrinx) is suspected as the root cause of the curve.

How Is It Treated?

Treatment is customized based on the child's age, how much growth they have left and the severity of the curve:

  • Observation ("Watch and Wait"): For mild curves, regular checkups and periodic X-rays every 6 months allow the specialist to monitor the spine closely during growth spurts.
  • Spinal Bracing: For moderate, active curves in growing children, a custom-fitted back brace can be worn under clothes. While a brace doesn't reverse an existing curve, it holds the spine straight to prevent the curve from getting worse as the child grows.
  • Physical Therapy & Exercise: Specialized physical therapy programs help build core strength, improve posture, and maintain flexibility around the spinal column.
  • Surgical Correction: For severe curves or rapidly progressing deformities, a neurosurgeon can perform spinal correction and fusion (or growth-friendly rod placements in younger children). Modern surgical techniques safely straighten the spine, balance the torso and preserve healthy posture.
