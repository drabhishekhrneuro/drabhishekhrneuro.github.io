Myelomeningocele Repair (Surgical Closure of Spina Bifida)

What Is Myelomeningocele Repair?

Imagine the developing spinal cord during early pregnancy as a flat strip of tissue that folds into a protective hollow tube—the neural tube—which later forms the spinal cord, brain, and overlying spinal column. When this neural tube fails to close completely during the first month of fetal development (a severe form of open spina bifida), a defect remains in the back bones and skin. The underlying spinal cord, protective spinal membranes (meninges), and spinal fluid protrude outward into a delicate, fluid-filled sac visible on the infant's back at birth.

Because the spinal cord is exposed to amniotic fluid in the womb and to air after delivery, it is highly vulnerable to progressive nerve damage and life-threatening bacterial infections like meningitis. Myelomeningocele Repair is a specialized pediatric neurosurgical operation to carefully release the exposed spinal cord, tuck it safely back inside the spinal canal, rebuild protective tissue layers around it, and achieve watertight skin closure.

Think of myelomeningocele repair as micro-restoration and sealing of the spinal cord shield:

  • Infection Defense Barrier: Closing the open spinal defect immediately after birth (or in utero via fetal surgery) isolates the central nervous system from environmental bacteria, preventing severe spinal fluid infections.
  • Preservation of Nerve Function: Gently untethering and repositioning delicate spinal cord tissue prevents further mechanical stretching and chemical injury, helping preserve existing leg movement, sensation, and bowel/bladder control.
  • Watertight Anatomical Reconstruction: Multi-layer microsurgical reconstruction recreates the natural protective barriers around the spinal cord, including the dural sac, muscle fascia, and outer skin.

When Is Myelomeningocele Repair Performed?

Myelomeningocele repair is an essential surgical intervention performed either immediately after birth or prenatally (fetal surgery):

Open Neural Tube Defect at Birth

An exposed, fluid-filled sac or open neural placode on the infant's lower back present at delivery, requiring urgent repair within 24 to 48 hours to prevent infection.

Prenatal Fetal Diagnosis (Fetal Repair Candidate)

Targeted mid-gestation ultrasound and fetal MRI identifying an open spina bifida defect between 19 and 26 weeks of pregnancy, qualifying for intrauterine repair.

Associated Hydrocephalus & Chiari II Malformation

Exposed spinal tissue causing leakage of spinal fluid, pulling the brainstem slightly downward into the spinal canal (Chiari II) and requiring integrated monitoring for cerebrospinal fluid accumulation (hydrocephalus).

Prevention of Progressive Neurological Deficits

Urgent surgical protection to safeguard lower limb motor function, pelvic floor reflexes, and preserve bladder/bowel nerve pathways.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Meticulous multidisciplinary planning begins prenatally or immediately in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU):

  • High-Resolution Fetal / Neonatal MRI: Evaluates the precise level and size of the spinal defect, assesses nerve root alignment, and checks brain structures for ventriculomegaly or Chiari II malformation.
  • Sterile Neonatal Care Protocols: Immediately after birth, the exposed sac is covered with sterile, saline-moistened non-adherent dressings and the infant is positioned face-down (prone) to prevent trauma or fluid leakage.
  • Multidisciplinary Care Coordination: Aligning pediatric neurosurgery, pediatric plastic surgery, neonatology, pediatric urology, and orthopedics to manage comprehensive long-term developmental needs.
  • Pediatric Anesthetic & Fluid Mapping: Setting up specialized temperature-controlled operating rooms and precise micro-vascular monitoring tailored for newborns or intrauterine fetal surgery.

What Happens During the Surgery?

Postnatal repair is performed under general anesthesia utilizing high-precision microsurgical techniques:

  • Stage 1: Position & Micro-Dissection: The infant is positioned face-down (prone). Working under a high-power operating microscope, the neurosurgeon delineates the boundary between healthy skin and the exposed neural placode.
  • Stage 2: Neural Placode Untethering & Re-tubulation: The exposed neural tissue is gently freed (untethered) from the surrounding skin edges and folded back into a rounded tubular structure resembling the natural spinal cord shape.
  • Stage 3: Reconstruction of the Dural Sleeve: The membrane lining (dura) is freed from surrounding tissues, wrapped smoothly around the reconstituted spinal cord, and closed with delicate micro-sutures to create a watertight, fluid-filled protective space.
  • Stage 4: Fascial & Muscle Layer Closure: Surrounding back muscle fascia is mobilized across the midline over the dural repair to provide a durable structural layer of tissue protection.
  • Stage 5: Cosmetic Watertight Skin Closure: The outer skin is mobilized (using pediatric plastic surgery rotational techniques for large defects) and closed seamlessly across the midline over surgical drains, ensuring a tension-free, watertight seal.

Post-Operative Recovery & Long-Term Care

Recovery involves intensive neonatal care, wound healing management, and long-term multidisciplinary follow-up:

  • NICU Monitoring & Prone Positioning (5 to 7 Days): The baby is cared for in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) and kept lying on their stomach or side to prevent direct pressure on the healing back incision.
  • Hydrocephalus & Head Circumference Surveillance: Daily measurement of head circumference and ultrasound checks of brain ventricles to monitor for spinal fluid buildup (hydrocephalus), which may require a ventricular shunt or ETV procedure.
  • Urological & Orthopedic Assessment: Evaluating bladder emptying function and lower limb alignment early to establish ongoing care plans with pediatric urologists and orthopedists.
  • Comprehensive Long-Term Multidisciplinary Care: Follow-up monitoring tracks spinal growth to watch for potential re-tethering (tethered cord syndrome), ensuring optimal physical, mobility, and developmental progress throughout childhood.
