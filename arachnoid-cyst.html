Intracranial Arachnoid Cyst +

What Is an Intracranial Arachnoid Cyst?

Your brain and spinal cord are surrounded and protected by three thin covering layers of tissue called membranes. The middle layer is called the arachnoid membrane (named because its delicate structure looks like a spiderweb).

Normally, protective clear fluid—called cerebrospinal fluid (CSF)—flows freely around and through these layers to cushion the brain.

An intracranial arachnoid cyst occurs when a small pocket or tear forms in this arachnoid membrane during early development, causing fluid to collect and form a fluid-filled pouch inside the skull. Think of it like a tiny, fluid-filled balloon sitting in the space around the brain. Most arachnoid cysts are harmless, non-cancerous, and stay the exact same size throughout life.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

The vast majority of arachnoid cysts never cause symptoms and are found purely by accident during a brain scan for something else (like an evaluation after a minor sports injury). However, if a cyst grows large enough to push against nearby brain tissue or block fluid flow, symptoms can develop:

Headaches

Persistent or recurrent headaches, often worse in the morning or when coughing/straining.

Seizures

Uncontrolled electrical activity in the brain caused by pressure on surrounding tissue.

Nausea & Vomiting

Caused by increased pressure inside the skull.

Developmental or Behavioral Changes

In children, a large cyst can occasionally lead to developmental delays, early puberty or subtle changes in motor skills.

Dizziness or Balance Issues

Unsteadiness while walking, depending on where the cyst is located inside the brain.

How is it Discovered?

Because arachnoid cysts often do not produce noticeable symptoms, they are usually identified using standard neuroimaging:

  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The preferred diagnostic scan. An MRI provides clear, detailed pictures showing the exact size, shape and location of the fluid pouch and how it relates to nearby brain structures.
  • Computed Tomography (CT) Scan: Often used in emergency settings to quickly identify fluid pockets and check for signs of pressure inside the skull.

How is it Treated?

Treatment depends entirely on whether the cyst is causing symptoms or interfering with brain function:

  • Observation ("Watch and Wait"): If the cyst is small, asymptomatic, and not placing pressure on the brain, no surgery is needed. Your neurosurgeon will simply monitor it with periodic neurological exams and occasional follow-up MRI scans.
  • Surgical Fenestration: If the cyst is large or causing symptoms, a surgeon can create small openings or "windows" in the wall of the cyst. This allows the trapped fluid to drain into the brain's natural fluid pathways, relieving pressure. This is often done using minimally invasive neurosurgical techniques.
  • Cyst Shunting: In select cases where fenestration isn't ideal, a tiny tube (shunt) is placed inside the cyst to drain the fluid safely into another part of the body, such as the abdomen.
