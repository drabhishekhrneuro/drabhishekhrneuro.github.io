Congenital Hydrocephalus

What Is Congenital Hydrocephalus?

Inside everyone’s brain, there are natural hollow spaces called ventricles that produce a clear, nutrient-rich fluid known as cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Think of CSF like a built-in plumbing and cushion system—it flows continuously around the brain and spinal cord, delivering nutrients, removing waste and protecting the brain from injury.

Under normal conditions, the body produces CSF, circulates it through the brain's pathways and then absorbs it back into the bloodstream in a perfectly balanced cycle.

Congenital Hydrocephalus occurs when a baby is born with a disruption in this fluid system—either because a drainage pathway is blocked, or because the body cannot absorb the fluid properly. As a result, CSF builds up inside the brain like water trapped behind a dam. Because an infant's skull bones are still soft and flexible, this extra fluid causes the head size to enlarge to accommodate the pressure.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

In infants, the signs of hydrocephalus are often visible on the outside because their skull bones haven't fully fused together yet:

Unusually Rapid Head Growth

A baby's head circumference growing much faster than average or appearing noticeably larger than normal.

A Bulging "Soft Spot" (Fontanelle)

The soft area on top of the baby's head feels firm, tense or visibly bulges outward.

"Sunset" Eye Appearance

The baby's eyes appear to look downward constantly, with the white part of the eye visible above the iris.

Irritability & Poor Feeding

Increased fussiness, unusual sleepiness, vomiting or difficulty in feeding due to pressure changes inside the head.

Seizures or High-Pitched Crying

In more advanced cases, increased pressure can trigger stiffness, seizures or a distinct, high-pitched cry.

How Is It Discovered?

Congenital hydrocephalus can often be detected before birth or diagnosed quickly after delivery:

  • Routine Prenatal Ultrasound: Many cases are first spotted during standard pregnancy scans, which show enlarged fluid spaces (ventricles) inside the developing brain.
  • Post-Birth Head Measurements: Routine monitoring of a newborn's head circumference during baby checkups provides an early warning if growth accelerates.
  • Ultrasound & Brain Scans (MRI/CT): Ultrasound can easily scan a baby's brain through the open soft spot. A follow-up MRI or CT scan provides detailed images of the brain's internal structure to pin down the exact location of any blockage.

How Is It Treated?

Treatment focuses on restoring normal fluid flow to relieve pressure and allow the brain to grow and develop normally.

  • VP Shunt Placement (Ventriculoperitoneal Shunt): The most common treatment. A neurosurgeon gently places a thin, flexible tube (a shunt) inside the brain's fluid spaces. The tube runs under the skin down to the abdomen, where the excess fluid is safely absorbed by the body. Think of it like installing a subtle internal drainage valve.
  • Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy (ETV): A minimally invasive option where the surgeon uses a tiny camera (endoscope) to make a small, natural opening in the floor of the brain's ventricle. This bypasses the blockage and allows fluid to drain naturally without needing a permanent implant.
  • Ongoing Developmental Support: With early diagnosis and effective fluid management, many children thrive. Long-term care involves regular neurosurgical checkups and physical or occupational therapy to support healthy development.
