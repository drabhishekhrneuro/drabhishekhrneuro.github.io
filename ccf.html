Carotid Cavernous Fistula (CCF)

What Is a Carotid Cavernous Fistula?

Imagine your brain's circulatory system as two separate plumbing lines running side by side: high-pressure arterial pipes bringing oxygenated blood up from the heart and low-pressure venous drainage channels (like the cavernous sinus behind your eyes) taking blood back.

A Carotid Cavernous Fistula (CCF) happens when an abnormal opening or tear forms between the carotid artery system and the cavernous sinus.

Because of this direct opening, high-pressure arterial blood bypasses its normal pathway and rushes directly into the delicate venous sinus network. This sudden influx of high-pressure blood causes venous congestion behind the eye, increasing pressure within the eye socket, impairing eye movement and risking vision loss if left untreated.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because the cavernous sinus surrounds the nerves and veins controlling the eye, symptoms primarily affect vision and eye appearance:

Pulsatile Proptosis (Bulging Eye)

A noticeable bulging or swelling of one eye that may visibly pulse in time with your heartbeat.

Redness and Swelling (Chemosis)

Severe redness, bloodshot appearance or swelling of the tissue lining the eyeball and eyelids due to backed-up blood flow.

Whooshing Sound (Bruit)

Hearing a rhythmic "whooshing" or humming sound in the ear that matches the beat of your pulse.

Double Vision (Diplopia)

Difficulty moving the eye normally or seeing double due to pressure on the cranial nerves passing through the cavernous sinus.

Eye Pain & Decreased Vision

Progressive eye pressure, aching pain behind the eye or blurred vision caused by elevated intraocular pressure.

How is it Discovered?

Diagnosing a CCF requires accurate imaging to evaluate the flow rate and exact location of the abnormal connection:

  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA): The gold-standard diagnostic procedure where contrast dye and high-speed X-ray imaging pinpoint the exact tear or leak between the carotid artery and cavernous sinus.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MRA: Non-invasive imaging that detects swelling in the eye socket muscles, dilated veins and checks for subtle changes in brain tissue.
  • CT Angiography (CTA): Rapid imaging that reveals dilated ophthalmic veins, enlarged cavernous sinuses and any associated facial or cranial fractures if caused by trauma.
  • Ophthalmological Exam: A thorough examination measuring intraocular pressure, eye movement and optic nerve health.

How is it Treated?

Treatment focuses on closing the abnormal connection to restore normal blood flow and protect vision:

  • Endovascular Embolization: The primary and most effective treatment, where a tiny catheter is guided through the blood vessels to seal the fistula using detachable coils, specialized glue or liquid embolic agents.
  • Covered Stents or Flow Diverters: Placing a mesh stent inside the carotid artery across the tear to redirect blood flow along its normal pathway while allowing the fistula to heal closed.
  • Observation & Conservative Management: For low-flow, indirect fistulas with minimal symptoms, your neurosurgeon may monitor the condition closely, as some small indirect fistulas can occasionally resolve on their own.
  • Post-Procedure Recovery: Follow-up eye exams and imaging to confirm that eye pressure returns to normal, double vision resolves and vision is fully preserved.
