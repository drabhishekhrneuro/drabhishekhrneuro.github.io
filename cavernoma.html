Brain Cavernomas (Cavernous Malformations)

What Is a Brain Cavernoma?

Imagine your brain's blood vessels as a smooth network of flexible pipes designed to keep blood flowing steadily without leaking.

A brain cavernoma (also known as a cavernous angioma, cavernous malformation or CCM) happens when a cluster of small, abnormal blood vessels forms in a compact, berry-like bundle inside the brain.

Unlike normal blood vessels, these abnormal clusters are composed of thin, fragile walls lined with weak tissue that lacks elasticity. Because these walls are thin and prone to micro-leaks, blood tends to pool, clot and slowly ooze into surrounding brain tissue. Over time, this slow leakage causes local irritation, brain tissue staining and pressure that can trigger neurological symptoms or sudden bleeding.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Many cavernomas remain small and unnoticed, but symptoms can emerge when they leak, grow or irritate adjacent brain tissue:

Seizures

The most common symptom, occurring when minor blood leakage irritates the surrounding electrical pathways of the brain.

Persistent or Recurrent Headaches

Frequent, localized headaches caused by minor swelling or small episodes of bleeding within the lesion.

Focal Weakness or Numbness

Sudden or gradual loss of strength, tingling or heaviness in an arm, leg or one side of the face.

Vision and Balance Problems

Double vision, blurred sight, dizziness or unsteadiness while walking, especially if the cavernoma is located in the brainstem or cerebellum.

Speech and Memory Difficulties

Trouble finding words, slurred speech or subtle cognitive changes depending on which area of the brain contains the lesion.

How is it Discovered?

Because cavernomas are low-pressure blood vessel clusters, they require specialized diagnostic imaging to detect clearly:

  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The absolute gold-standard test. Specialized MRI sequences (such as Gradient Echo or SWI) reveal the characteristic "popcorn-like" appearance of the cavernoma and detect surrounding iron deposits from past micro-leaks.
  • Family History Assessment: Genetic screening or consultation may be recommended if multiple cavernomas are found, as a subset of cavernomas are inherited (Familial Cavernous Malformations).
  • Computed Tomography (CT) Scan: A fast scan that helps identify calcifications or acute, larger bleeds when a patient presents with sudden severe symptoms.

How is it Treated?

Treatment strategies are highly individualized based on the cavernoma’s location, whether it has bled and the severity of symptoms:

  • Conservative Management & Observation: For quiet, incidental cavernomas that cause no symptoms or bleeds, your neurosurgeon may recommend routine follow-up MRI scans to monitor for changes.
  • Microsurgical Resection: The primary cure for symptomatic or recurring cavernomas. A skilled neurosurgeon uses high-precision micro-instruments to carefully detach and completely remove the cavernoma bundle, protecting surrounding healthy brain tissue.
  • Seizure Management: Anti-seizure medications prescribed to effectively control and prevent electrical disruptions caused by local brain tissue irritation.
  • Rehabilitation & Recovery Support: Post-surgical physical, occupational or speech therapy tailored to help rebuild strength, balance and coordination during recovery.
