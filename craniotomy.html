Craniotomy for Brain Tumour

What Is a Craniotomy for Brain Tumour?

Imagine your skull as a protective vault that safeguards your brain. When a tumour develops inside this vault, accessing it requires creating a temporary, controlled opening through the bone. A craniotomy is the foundational neurosurgical procedure where a section of the skull bone (a "bone flap") is carefully removed to give the neurosurgeon direct access to the brain and tumour.

During a craniotomy for a brain tumour, the surgical team uses high-precision microsurgical tools, intraoperative navigation, and operating microscopes to safely remove (resect) the tumour or obtain a tissue biopsy, after which the bone flap is securely replaced using specialized titanium micro-plates and screws.

Think of a craniotomy as creating a temporary, precision access hatch to work inside a delicate environment:

  • Direct & Guided Access: Modern craniotomies are highly targeted. Advanced GPS-like computer navigation (neuronavigation) maps the exact location of the tumour beforehand, allowing the bone opening to be as small and precisely placed as possible.
  • Surgical Goals: The primary objectives are to safely remove as much tumour tissue as possible (maximal safe resection), relieve intracranial pressure, alleviate neurological symptoms, and obtain exact tissue samples for molecular and pathological diagnosis.
  • Advanced Safety Technologies: Today's brain tumour surgeries frequently utilize intraoperative ultrasound, fluorescence-guided visualization (e.g., 5-ALA or sodium fluorescein), intraoperative MRI, and continuous nerve monitoring to protect healthy brain pathways.

When Is a Craniotomy Recommended?

A craniotomy is recommended when imaging confirms a brain mass that requires surgical removal, reduction of pressure, or definitive histological diagnosis:

Primary Brain Tumours

Indicated for benign or malignant tumours originating in brain tissue or its coverings, including gliomas, meningiomas, acoustic neuromas, and ependymomas.

Metastatic Brain Tumours

Recommended for single or well-defined multiple tumours that have spread to the brain from cancers originating elsewhere in the body (such as lung, breast, or skin).

Elevated Intracranial Pressure & Mass Effect

When an expanding tumour squeezes surrounding brain tissue, causing severe headaches, persistent nausea, progressive weakness, or impending brain herniation.

Uncontrolled Seizures or Neurological Deficits

When a tumour triggers drug-resistant seizures, progressive limb weakness, speech difficulty, or visual changes that can be improved by surgical decompression.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Surgical success depends on precise preoperative mapping to tailor the safest approach for each individual tumour:

  • High-Resolution Neuronavigation MRI: Specialized 3D MRI scans with intravenous contrast are loaded directly into the surgical navigation computer, creating a virtual 3D map of the patient's brain and tumour.
  • Functional MRI (fMRI) & Tractography (DTI): Advanced imaging used when tumours lie near critical brain areas (eloquent cortex) to map out essential motor, speech, and visual nerve pathways surrounding the tumour.
  • Preoperative Medical Optimization: Administration of anti-swelling medications (such as corticosteroids) to reduce brain edema around the tumour, along with anti-seizure medications if indicated.
  • Anesthetic Evaluation & Planning: Comprehensive safety screening to plan general anesthesia or specialized "awake craniotomy" protocols when real-time language or movement testing during surgery is required.

What Happens During the Surgery?

A craniotomy for brain tumour removal involves several precise, sequential stages conducted under strict microsurgical standards:

  • Stage 1: Anesthesia & Precision Positioning: The patient is made completely comfortable under general anesthesia (or prepared for awake monitoring). The head is stabilized in a specialized frame to ensure millimeter-accurate alignment with the neuronavigation system.
  • Stage 2: Scalp Incision & Bone Flap Creation: A discreet scalp incision is made, usually hidden within the hairline. A small section of bone (the bone flap) is carefully created using specialized micro-drills and temporarily lifted out.
  • Stage 3: Opening the Dura & Microsurgical Resection: The protective outer brain membrane (dura mater) is opened. Working under a high-power operating microscope or endoscope, the neurosurgeon meticulously separates the tumour from healthy brain tissue and blood vessels, frequently utilizing ultrasonic aspirators (CUSA) to gently debulk the tumour.
  • Stage 4: Intraoperative Monitoring & Fluorescence Guidance: Real-time electrical nerve signals (IONM) are monitored to ensure motor and sensory tracts remain protected. Specialized fluorescent dyes may be illuminated under blue light to distinguish microscopic tumour margins from healthy brain tissue.
  • Stage 5: Closure & Bone Reconstruction: Once maximum safe removal is complete, meticulous bleeding control (hemostasis) is performed. The dura is watertight-closed, the original bone flap is securely reattached using small titanium plates and screws, and the scalp is closed with cosmetic sutures or staples.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery after a craniotomy is a gradual, carefully monitored process structured to support brain healing, restore strength, and ensure maximum long-term safety:

  • Neuro-ICU Monitoring (First 24–48 Hours): Immediately following surgery, patients are closely monitored in a specialized Neurosurgical Intensive Care Unit (ICU) for frequent neurological checks, blood pressure management, and pain control.
  • Early Hospital Care & Mobilization: Patients typically transition to a standard neurosurgical ward within 1–2 days. Early, assisted walking is encouraged to promote circulation, while post-operative imaging (MRI or CT) is performed to confirm complete tumor resection and rule out swelling or bleeding.
  • Incision & Wound Care: The scalp incision is kept clean and dry. Sutures or staples are usually removed in the clinic around 10 to 14 days after surgery. Mild head swelling or bruising around the eyes and forehead is common and subsides gradually.
  • Inpatient or Outpatient Rehabilitation: Depending on the location of the tumour, physical therapy, occupational therapy, or speech therapy may begin in the hospital to retrain motor skills, speech, balance, and cognitive function.
  • Histology Review & Multi-Disciplinary Planning: Tissue removed during surgery undergoes comprehensive pathological and molecular testing. Once final results are available (usually within 5–7 days), a personalized follow-up plan—which may include monitoring, targeted therapies, radiation, or chemotherapy—is discussed with the multidisciplinary neuro-oncology team.
