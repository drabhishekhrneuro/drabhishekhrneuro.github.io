Anterior Cranial Fossa (ACF) Base Carpeting & CSF Leak Repair

What Is ACF Base Carpeting?

Imagine the floor of your anterior skull base—the bony shelf supporting your frontal lobes right above your eye sockets and nasal cavity—as a critical anatomical ceiling. When complex or multiple cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leaks occur across this floor, or following major anterior skull base trauma, transnasal approaches alone may not suffice.

Anterior Cranial Fossa (ACF) base carpeting is an open neurosurgical procedure utilizing a bifrontal craniotomy (a surgical window across the forehead behind the hairline). Through this wide exposure, a robust biological graft—such as a vascularized pericranial flap or autologous fascia lata—is precisely laid and meticulously sutured directly to the dura mater across the entire anterior skull floor.

Think of this procedure as open structural reinforcement for a widespread skull base defect:

  • Direct Open Visualization: A bifrontal approach provides unhindered, direct line-of-sight access to the entire anterior cranial fossa floor, cribriform plate, and planum sphenoidale.
  • Surgical Sutured Fixation: Unlike purely endoscopic inlays, open ACF carpeting allows the neurosurgeon to physically suture the graft directly to the dural edges, ensuring absolute mechanical stability under high or fluctuating brain pressures.
  • Vascularized vs. Free Grafts: Utilizing a pedicled pericranial flap (harvested from the scalp with its own blood supply) or an autologous fascia lata graft provides living, durable biological tissue that promotes rapid healing and permanently seals off nasal pathways.

When Is This Procedure Indicated?

This open cranial approach is reserved for complex anterior skull base disruptions where minimally invasive techniques are inadequate:

Widespread or Complex Traumatic Skull Base Fractures

Severe comminuted fractures of the cribriform plate, frontal sinus posterior wall, or roof of the ethmoid sinuses with persistent fluid leakage.

Failed Previous Endoscopic Repair Attempts

Recurrent CSF leaks where prior transnasal patches have failed or where tissue scarring prevents reliable endoscopic sealing.

Large Basal Meningoencephaloceles or Skull Base Defects

Congenital or acquired large bony defects where brain tissue and membranes herniate into the nasal sinuses.

Simultaneous Frontal Sinus Pathology or Tumours

When anterior skull base reconstruction must be combined with the removal of frontal sinus tumours, extensive mucoceles, or anterior brain lesions.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Detailed preoperative imaging and structural mapping ensure precise execution of the bifrontal approach:

  • High-Resolution Thin-Slice CT Sinus & Brain: Sub-millimeter bony imaging to map every fracture line, bone defect, and sinus pneumatization pattern across the anterior fossa.
  • Brain MRI & MR Cisternography: Specialized sequences confirming the exact site of CSF leakage and evaluating frontal lobe status.
  • Pericranial Flap Feasibility Assessment: Evaluating the thickness and blood supply of the scalp pericranium to ensure it can be safely harvested as a vascularized flap.
  • Systemic & Anesthetic Optimization: Pre-surgical evaluation ensuring patient stability, infection screening, and management of intracranial pressure.

What Happens During the Surgery?

The procedure is executed under operating microscope visualization through careful, multi-staged surgical phases:

  • Stage 1: Scalp Incision & Pericranial Harvest: A bicoronal scalp incision is made safely hidden behind the hairline. Before opening the skull, a large, living sheet of connective tissue (the pericranium) based on the supraorbital and supratrochlear blood vessels is carefully harvested and reflected forward.
  • Stage 2: Bifrontal Craniotomy: A frontal bone flap is meticulously cut across the forehead just above the eyebrows and eye sockets, exposing the anterior cranial fossa floor from side to side.
  • Stage 3: Intradural Exploration & Defect Identification: The frontal lobes are gently elevated under the operating microscope, and the dura covering the anterior skull base is inspected to identify all tear sites, bone sharp edges, and CSF leak points.
  • Stage 4: Graft Carpeting & Direct Suturing: The harvested pericranial flap (or prepared fascia lata graft) is draped across the floor of the anterior cranial fossa. The surgeon meticulously sutures the graft edge-to-edge directly to the dural margins using fine, non-absorbable sutures to create a permanent, watertight carpet over the defect.
  • Stage 5: Reconstruction & Closure: The frontal bone flap is secured back in place using titanium mini-plates and screws, and the scalp incision is closed carefully over surgical drains.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery following open bifrontal skull base repair requires structured critical care and careful management of intracranial pressure:

  • Neuro-ICU Care (First 24–48 Hours): Close monitoring in a specialized neuro-intensive care unit for neurological alertness, vital signs, and management of temporary frontal lobe swelling.
  • Pressure Precautions & Activity Limits: Strict avoidance of nose blowing, heavy straining, coughing, or bending forward to protect the sutured skull base graft while biological healing takes place.
  • Temporary Lumbar Drain (Optional): A spinal drain may be utilized for a few days to divert CSF fluid and minimize pressure against the newly repaired anterior skull base floor.
  • Long-Term Follow-Up: Periodic clinical evaluations and follow-up neuroimaging to confirm complete cessation of the leak and proper healing of the bifrontal surgical site.
