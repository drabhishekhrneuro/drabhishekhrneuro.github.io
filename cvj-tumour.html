Craniovertebral Junction (CVJ) Tumours

What Is a Craniovertebral Junction (CVJ) Tumour?

Imagine your head and neck as a high-precision mechanical joint where the base of your skull connects directly to the top of your spinal column. The Craniovertebral Junction (CVJ) encompasses the occipital bone at the skull base, the first neck vertebra (Atlas/C1), and the second neck vertebra (Axis/C2 with its peg-like dens).

A CVJ tumour is an abnormal growth—either benign or malignant—developing within this critical transition zone where the brainstem tapers into the upper spinal cord.

Think of the CVJ as the master gateway between your brain and body:

  • High-Stakes Anatomy: Essential structures pass through or directly adjacent to this tight gateway, including the lower brainstem (medulla oblongata), upper cervical spinal cord, major vertebral arteries delivering blood to the brain, and lower cranial nerves controlling swallowing, speech, and shoulder movement.
  • Diverse Tumour Origins: CVJ tumours can originate from bones (chordomas, chondrosarcomas, osteomas), protective linings (meningiomas), nerve sheaths (schwannomas, neurofibromas), embryonic remnants (epidermoid cysts), or spread from cancers elsewhere in the body (metastases).
  • Dual Threat: Because space within the skull base opening (foramen magnum) is extremely limited, expanding tumours threaten both neurological function (spinal cord compression) and mechanical stability (neck movement).

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms often develop slowly and can be complex, reflecting pressure on the upper spinal cord, brainstem, or lower cranial nerves:

Suboccipital Neck Pain & Stiffness

Persistent, deep aching pain at the base of the skull and upper neck, often worsening when tilting, turning, or extending the head.

Progressive Limb Weakness & Clumsiness (Myelopathy)

Gradual heaviness, weakness, or stiffness starting in the hands or legs, leading to difficulty buttoning clothes, frequent stumbling, or gait unsteadiness.

Numbness & Loss of Sensation

"Pins and needles," loss of temperature awareness, or reduced feeling spreading across the shoulders, arms, or trunk.

Swallowing & Speech Difficulties (Lower Cranial Nerve Signs)

Difficulty swallowing liquids or solids (dysphagia), hoarseness, slurred speech, or tongue weakness caused by pressure on lower cranial nerves.

Lhermitte's Sign & Balance Issues

An electric shock-like sensation shooting down the spine when bending the neck forward, accompanied by dizziness or loss of balance.

How is it Discovered?

Precise diagnostic mapping of the bony, vascular, and neural elements around the skull base is critical for surgical planning:

  • High-Resolution Contrast MRI: The cornerstone diagnostic scan. Multi-planar MRI clearly details the tumour size, soft tissue origin, relationship to the medulla and spinal cord, and degree of neural compression.
  • 3D Bone CT Scan: High-definition CT imaging used to evaluate bony destruction at the C1-C2 joint, check for bone involvement (as seen in chordomas), and assess overall structural stability of the skull base.
  • CT Angiography (CTA) / MR Angiography (MRA): Specialized vascular imaging performed to map the precise course of the vertebral arteries relative to the tumour to ensure safe surgical dissection.
  • Dynamic Flexion-Extension X-rays: Bending X-rays evaluated to check whether the tumour or bone erosion has compromised the stability of the skull-neck joint.

How is it Treated?

Treatment requires specialized skull base expertise, balancing complete tumour removal with protecting vital brainstem pathways and neck stability:

  • Endoscopic Endonasal / Transoral Approach: A modern, minimally invasive approach through the nose or back of the throat to reach and remove anterior CVJ tumours (such as chordomas or clival meningiomas) without visible external incisions.
  • Posterolateral / Far-Lateral Craniotomy: High-precision neurosurgery performed around the side or back of the skull base, providing direct access to tumours compressing the side or back of the brainstem and spinal cord.
  • Occipitocervical Fixation & Fusion: Surgical stabilization placing supportive screws and rods from the base of the skull to the upper neck vertebrae if tumour removal or bone destruction creates joint instability.
  • Stereotactic Radiosurgery (Gamma Knife / CyberKnife / Proton Beam): Precision radiation used to target residual, deep, or bone-invasive tumour remnants (like chordomas or chondrosarcomas) while sparing adjacent spinal cord tissue.
  • Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM): Continuous real-time electrical monitoring of brainstem, motor, and sensory pathways throughout surgery to maximize patient safety.
