Tethered Cord Syndrome

What Is Tethered Cord Syndrome?

Imagine your spinal cord as a bunch of wires running down inside your spine. Under normal conditions, as you stretch, bend or grow taller, this cord slides smoothly up and down within the spinal canal without any tension.

Tethered Cord Syndrome happens when the bottom end of the spinal cord becomes abnormally attached or "stuck" to the surrounding tissues, fat or scar tissue at the base of the spine.

As a child grows taller—or as an adult bends and stretches—this attachment pulls on the spinal cord like a stretched rubber band. Over time, this constant tugging restricts blood flow to the nerves and causes progressive nerve strain, leading to pain, movement difficulty and changes in bowel & bladder function.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because the bottom of the spinal cord controls the lower body, symptoms typically show up in the legs, back and pelvic organs. :

Lower Back or Leg Pain

A constant dull ache in the lower back that often shoots down one or both legs, frequently made worse by bending or active exercises.

Leg Weakness

Difficulty in walking, frequent tripping or noticing that one leg or foot appears smaller or weaker than the other.

Bladder & Bowel Control Changes

Sudden onset of bedwetting in a previously trained child, frequent urinary tract infections or difficulty controlling bladder and bowel movements.

Skin Marks on the Lower Back

In children, a tethered cord is often accompanied by subtle marks present from birth over the lower spine—such as a deep dimple, a tuft of hair, a small fatty lump, or a patch of discolored skin.

Foot and Spine Changes

Developing high-arched feet, clawed toes or an unusual curve in the spine (scoliosis) as the body attempts to relieve tension on the spinal cord.

How is it Discovered?

Catching a tethered cord early helps protect nerve function before permanent stretching occurs:

  • Physical & Neurological Exam: Checking muscle strength, leg reflexes, flexibility and examining the lower back for birthmarks or dimples.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): An MRI scan of the spine is the gold-standard test. It clearly shows the exact position of the bottom of the spinal cord (the conus medullaris) and reveals what tissue is causing it to stick.
  • Urodynamic Studies: Specialized, painless tests that evaluate how well the bladder stores and releases urine, which helps detect early nerve strain before physical weakness starts.

How is it Treated?

The main goal of treatment is to relieve the tension on the spinal cord and prevent further nerve irritation.

  • Tethered Cord Release Surgery: A precise neurosurgical procedure where the surgeon carefully opens the area around the lower spine and separates the spinal cord from the scar tissue, fat or tight band holding it down. Once freed, the spinal cord relaxes back into its natural, tension-free state.
  • Monitoring & Observation: In mild cases where a tethered cord is discovered incidentally and causes no symptoms, your neurosurgeon may recommend regular follow-up scans and exams to monitor for changes before deciding on surgery.
  • Rehabilitation & Recovery: After surgical release, physical therapy helps rebuild leg strength, improve gait and assist recovery, while pediatric urology support ensures bladder health returns to normal.
