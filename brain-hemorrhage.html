Brain Hemorrhage (Intracerebral Hemorrhage)

What Is a Brain Hemorrhage?

Imagine your brain’s blood vessels as a smooth network of pressurized pipes delivering oxygen and vital nutrients directly to brain cells. Under normal conditions, these vessel walls are strong and elastic, keeping blood safely contained within the circulation.

A brain hemorrhage (also known as a hemorrhagic stroke or intracerebral hemorrhage) occurs when a weakened or damaged blood vessel inside the brain ruptures, causing blood to leak directly into surrounding brain tissue.

Because the skull is a rigid, non-expandable bony vault, blood leaking into brain tissue creates a double threat: it starves nearby brain cells of oxygen while creating a expanding collection of blood (a hematoma) that increases pressure within the head, compressing delicate brain structures.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms of a brain hemorrhage usually appear suddenly without warning and rapidly worsen as pressure builds inside the skull:

Sudden, Severe "Thunderclap" Headache

An extraordinarily intense, painful headache that reaches peak severity within seconds, often accompanied by neck stiffness.

Nausea and Projectile Vomiting

Sudden, uncontrollable nausea and vomiting triggered by a rapid increase in intracranial pressure.

One-Sided Weakness or Paralysis

Sudden weakness, numbness, or inability to move an arm, leg, or one side of the face.

Seizures

Unprovoked electrical fits or full-body convulsions caused by blood irritating sensitive cortical brain tissue.

Speech, Vision, or Balance Disturbances

Sudden slurring of words, inability to speak, loss of vision in one or both eyes, or severe loss of balance.

Confusion or Loss of Consciousness

Rapidly declining alertness, extreme drowsiness, confusion, or sudden loss of consciousness as pressure on the brainstem increases.

How is it Discovered?

Immediate, high-priority emergency neuroimaging is critical to locate the bleed, measure its size, and pinpoint the underlying cause:

  • Non-Contrast CT Scan: The immediate, first-line emergency scan used to quickly detect fresh blood inside the brain, determine hematoma volume, and assess swelling.
  • CT Angiography (CTA): A fast contrast-enhanced scan used to map out brain blood vessels, identify active bleeding ("spot sign"), and check for underlying aneurysms or vascular malformations.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MRA: Detailed structural imaging that evaluates surrounding brain tissue, detects microscopic past bleeds, and checks for cavernomas or underlying tumors.
  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA): The precise diagnostic catheter procedure used when CT or MRI scans suspect an underlying vascular abnormality like an AVM, aneurysm, or dural fistula.

How is it Treated?

Emergency medical and surgical management focuses on stopping the bleed, reducing pressure inside the skull, and protecting healthy brain tissue:

  • Emergency Medical Stabilization: Immediate blood pressure control using IV medications, rapid reversal of any blood-thinner (anticoagulant) medications, and management of brain swelling in an intensive care setting.
  • Minimally Invasive Hematoma Evacuation: Advanced neurosurgical techniques using small keyhole openings or stereotactic catheters to gently suction out or drain the blood collection and relieve pressure.
  • Surgical Craniotomy for Decompression: A neurosurgical procedure where a portion of the skull is temporarily removed (decompressive craniotomy) to allow swollen brain tissue room to expand while removing a large, life-threatening blood clot.
  • Treatment of Underlying Vascular Causes: Surgical clipping, endovascular coiling, or embolization performed to permanently seal off ruptured aneurysms or vascular malformations that caused the bleed.
  • Neuro-Rehabilitation: Specialized post-acute physical, occupational, and speech therapy aimed at rebuilding strength, restoring lost function, and supporting long-term brain recovery.
