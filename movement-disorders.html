Movement Disorders (Parkinson’s Disease, Essential Tremor & Dystonia)

What Are Movement Disorders?

Imagine your brain’s motor system as a finely tuned control console, where deep structures known as the basal ganglia act as the central movement filter. This internal circuit continuously refines muscle commands, ensuring that intentional movements are smooth, coordinated, and fluid while suppressing unintended, unwanted muscle activity.

Movement disorders are a group of neurological conditions caused by disruption or degeneration within these deep brain control circuits, leading to involuntary movements or difficulty executing fluid actions.

Think of your brain's motor circuit as experiencing a fine-tuning control disruption:

  • Circuit Dysregulation: Chemical imbalances (such as dopamine depletion in Parkinson’s disease) or abnormal electrical oscillatory loops lead to faulty signaling between the basal ganglia, thalamus, and motor cortex.
  • Major Subtypes:
    • Parkinson’s Disease: Progressive degeneration of dopamine-producing neurons, causing resting tremors, slowness of movement, and body stiffness.
    • Essential Tremor: A rhythmic shaking—most commonly affecting the hands and arms—that occurs during purposeful actions like writing or holding a cup (action tremor).
    • Dystonia: Involuntary, sustained muscle contractions that cause twisting movements, abnormal postures, or painful spasms in specific muscle groups or across the body.
  • Functional Quality of Life Impact: While these conditions rarely cause direct sudden paralysis, progressive motor symptoms interfere with daily activities like writing, eating, walking, and independent self-care.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms vary depending on the specific movement disorder, ranging from rhythmic tremors to profound muscle stiffness and slowness:

Tremors (Resting vs. Action Tremors)

Rhythmic shaking in the hands, arms, chin, or voice. In Parkinson's disease, tremors typically appear at rest ("pill-rolling"), whereas in Essential Tremor, shaking intensifies during active tasks like holding a spoon or reaching for objects.

Bradykinesia (Slowness of Movement)

A cardinal sign of Parkinson's: progressive slowing of spontaneous movements, making simple tasks like buttoning a shirt, getting out of a chair, or walking take significantly longer.

Muscle Rigidity & Stiffness

Persistent tightness or resistance in the limbs or trunk ("cogwheel rigidity"), leading to muscle aches, stooped posture, and a loss of natural arm swing while walking.

Dystonic Posturing & Involuntary Spasms

Uncontrolled, painful twisting or sustained contractions of specific body parts, such as neck tilting (cervical dystonia), forced eye blinking (blepharospasm), or foot cramping.

Postural Instability & Gait Freezing

Impaired balance leading to frequent stumbles, a wide or shuffling stride, or sudden "freezing of gait," where the feet feel momentarily glued to the floor when passing through doorways or turning.

How is it Discovered?

Diagnosing movement disorders relies on expert clinical neurological evaluation combined with advanced structural and functional neuroimaging:

  • Comprehensive Movement Disorder Neurological Exam: Detailed clinical scoring (such as the UPDRS rating scale) evaluating tremor characteristics, muscle tone, movement speed, gait dynamics, and response to trial medications (e.g., levodopa challenge test).
  • High-Resolution 3T Brain MRI: Thin-slice brain MRI performed to rule out structural causes of secondary parkinsonism or movement symptoms, such as stroke, normal pressure hydrocephalus, vascular malformations, or brain tumors.
  • Dopamine Transporter Scan (DaTscan / SPECT): Specialized nuclear imaging that visualizes dopamine transporter density in the striatum, helping distinguish true neurodegenerative parkinsonian syndromes from essential tremor or drug-induced symptoms.
  • Genetic Testing: Selected blood or saliva testing evaluated in early-onset parkinsonism, familial essential tremor, or hereditary dystonia syndromes to identify underlying gene mutations.

How is it Treated?

Treatment combines optimized medical therapy with advanced functional neurosurgical interventions to restore fluid movement and quality of life:

  • Targeted Pharmacotherapy: First-line medical management tailored to the condition, such as dopamine replacement therapy (Levodopa/Carbidopa) or dopamine agonists for Parkinson’s, and beta-blockers or primidone for Essential Tremor.
  • Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS): A transformative functional neurosurgical procedure where ultra-thin electrodes are precisely implanted into deep brain targets—such as the Subthalamic Nucleus (STN), Globus Pallidus Internus (GPi), or Ventral Intermediate Nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus. Connected to a pulse generator under the collarbone, DBS delivers continuous electrical impulses to override abnormal circuit signals and dramatically reduce tremors, rigidity, and dyskinesias.
  • MRI-Guided Focused Ultrasound (MRgFUS) / Thalamotomy: A incisionless, non-invasive functional procedure using focused ultrasound energy guided by real-time MRI to create a microscopic lesion in the thalamus (VIM), providing immediate, permanent relief from severe drug-resistant essential tremor or parkinsonian tremor.
  • Botulinum Toxin Injections: Targeted intramuscular injections under electromyographic (EMG) guidance to temporarily relax overactive muscles in focal dystonias (such as cervical dystonia or writer's cramp).
  • Targeted Physical, Occupational & Speech Therapy: Specialized motor rehabilitation programs (such as LSVT BIG and LOUD therapy) to train stride length, balance confidence, posture, and voice amplification.
