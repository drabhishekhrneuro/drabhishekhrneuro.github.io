Frequently Asked Questions

1. Do I need a doctor's referral to schedule an appointment?

While a formal referral from a general practitioner or medical specialist is helpful, it is not strictly required to schedule an initial consultation. If you are experiencing persistent neurological symptoms or have existing MRI/CT imaging, you may book an appointment directly.

2. How can I obtain a second opinion on a recommended brain or spine surgery?

We routinely provide thorough, objective second opinions for complex brain and spine recommendations. To arrange a second-opinion evaluation, in the "Contact" page go to "Request a Second Medical Opinion" and submit your complete diagnostic history—including high-resolution digital imaging scans (MRI/CT), written radiology reports, and previous clinic notes. We will independently review your diagnostic profile, verify whether surgery is indicated, and discuss all viable surgical, minimally invasive, or conservative alternatives.

3. How do I send my MRI or CT scans electronically before my visit?

You can upload your diagnostic imaging securely prior to your consultation. We accept digital scan files via cloud-based patient upload portals, secure DICOM file-sharing links, or direct electronic transfers from partner diagnostic centers. You can share file links and images on our Whatsapp number and we will contact you soon.

4. What happens during a first neurosurgical consultation?

Your initial consultation is a comprehensive clinical evaluation. We begin with a detailed review of your medical history, symptom progression, and prior treatments, followed by a focused neurological physical examination (evaluating strength, reflexes, sensory function, and coordination). Next, we perform an in-depth, side-by-side review of your MRI or CT scans on high-definition displays, explaining the exact underlying anatomy in clear, accessible terms. Finally, we discuss diagnostic conclusions and outline a tailored, step-by-step treatment plan.

5. Are virtual or online video consultations available?

Yes. We offer secure, high-definition tele-consultation services for initial reviews, second opinions, and follow-up evaluations. Virtual visits are especially convenient for out-of-town patients or those with mobility challenges. Once your imaging CDs or electronic scans are uploaded to our secure portal, we conduct a comprehensive video review to discuss your symptoms, analyze your imaging live on screen, and determine if an in-person physical examination or intervention is necessary.

6. Do you treat international or out-of-town patients?

Yes. We provide dedicated care protocols for out-of-town and international patients traveling for advanced neurosurgical and spine care. Our care team assists with remote pre-evaluation, digital imaging reviews, streamlined surgical scheduling, local hospital stay coordination, and post-operative follow-up planning to minimize unnecessary travel while ensuring seamless, continuous care.

7. Is open surgery always required for brain and spine conditions?

No. Open surgery is considered only when strictly necessary. A significant number of brain, spine, and nerve conditions can be managed non-operatively through structured physical rehabilitation, targeted pain interventions, medication, or careful clinical observation. When surgical intervention is required, advanced minimally invasive spine (MIS) techniques, micro-dissection corridors, stereotactic radiosurgery, or endovascular neurointerventions are used whenever appropriate to achieve optimal clinical outcomes with minimal anatomical disruption.

8. What is the difference between open surgery and minimally invasive surgery?

Traditional open surgery utilizes longer skin incisions and muscular detachment to provide direct, wide visual exposure of the target anatomy. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) achieves the exact same surgical objective inside the body through tiny skin punctures, operating microscopes/endoscopes, and specialized tubular retractors that gently slide between muscle fibers rather than cutting them. This results in significantly less tissue trauma, lower intraoperative blood loss, minimal post-operative pain, shorter hospital stays, and faster functional recovery.

9. What is intraoperative neuromonitoring (IONM) and how does it protect nerve function?

Intraoperative Neuromonitoring (IONM) is an advanced safety system that tracks the real-time health and electrical conduction of your nervous system throughout surgery. Dedicated neurophysiology specialists continuously monitor sensory and motor nerve signals—such as Somatosensory Evoked Potentials (SSEP), Motor Evoked Potentials (MEP), and continuous electromyography (EMG). If surgical manipulation approaches a sensitive nerve or spinal pathway, IONM immediately alerts the surgical team, ensuring maximum protection of delicate nerve structures.

10. How long will I need to stay in the hospital after surgery?

Hospital stay duration depends directly on the complexity of the procedure and your individual recovery rate. Outpatient procedures (such as endoscopic carpal tunnel release, simple nerve biopsies, or select minimally invasive discectomies) allow patients to return home the same day. Standard minimally invasive spine or uncomplicated cranial procedures typically require 1 to 3 days of inpatient observation, while complex cranial, vascular, or multi-level reconstruction surgeries may involve 3 to 7 days of structured post-operative monitoring and inpatient rehabilitation.

11. How do I know if my headache requires evaluation by a neurospecialist?

While most headaches are primary disorders (such as tension or migraine headaches), prompt neurospecialist evaluation is recommended if you experience any "red flag" symptoms. These include a sudden, extremely severe headache (often described as a "thunderclap" or the worst headache of your life), headaches accompanied by neurological deficits (such as limb weakness, numbness, visual loss, double vision, or speech difficulty), new-onset headaches after age 50, or headaches that progressively worsen over time and are triggered by coughing, bending, or straining.

12. Will I experience memory or cognitive changes after brain tumor surgery?

Preserving baseline cognitive function, memory, speech, and motor control is the highest priority during brain tumor surgery. Preoperative high-resolution functional MRI (fMRI) and intraoperative neuronavigation map critical functional centers down to the millimeter. During surgery, high-magnification microsurgical technique, continuous neuromonitoring, and (cuando appropriate) awake mapping protocols are utilized to remove tumor tissue safely while safeguarding speech and memory pathways. Transient fatigue or mild cognitive slowness can occur immediately post-op due to tissue healing, but this generally improves steadily over time.

13. What is endovascular neurointervention, and how does it treat stroke or aneurysm from inside the blood vessels?

Endovascular neurointervention is an advanced, minimally invasive approach to treating brain vascular conditions from inside the circulatory system without opening the skull. Under high-resolution fluoroscopic X-ray guidance, a micro-catheter is inserted into an artery at the wrist or upper thigh and threaded through the vascular tree into the brain. Neuro-interventionalists utilize this internal access corridor to deploy micro-coils, stents, or flow diverters to seal off brain aneurysms, repair vascular malformations (AVMs), or remove acute blood clots causing an ischemic stroke (thrombectomy).

14. When is surgery necessary for a herniated disc or sciatica?

Surgery is typically considered when severe leg pain, numbness, or weakness caused by a herniated disc (sciatica) fails to improve after 6 to 8 weeks of comprehensive conservative management—such as targeted physical therapy, anti-inflammatory medications, and epidural steroid injections. However, immediate emergency surgery is indicated if there is rapid, progressive leg weakness (such as foot drop) or sudden loss of bowel/bladder control (Cauda Equina Syndrome).

15. What is Minimally Invasive Spine (MIS) surgery, and what are its main advantages?

Minimally Invasive Spine (MIS) surgery utilizes specialized tubular retractors, micro-endoscopes, and high-precision intraoperative imaging to treat spinal pathology through small keyhole incisions. By dilating and preserving muscle tissue rather than stripping muscle away from bone, MIS surgery offers significant clinical benefits: substantially reduced operative blood loss, lower post-operative pain, reduced scar tissue formation, lower infection rates, shorter hospital stays, and a much faster return to daily activities.

16. How does spinal fusion differ from a simple decompression (laminectomy/discectomy)?

Spinal decompression (such as a discectomy or laminectomy) is a targeted procedure that removes localized bone spurs, thickened ligaments, or herniated disc material to relieve physical pressure on pinched spinal nerves without altering the underlying spinal architecture. Spinal fusion, on the other hand, is performed when spinal instability, abnormal alignment (spondylolisthesis), or severe spinal deformity exists; it permanently joins two or more vertebrae using bone grafts and specialized hardware (pedicle screws and rods) to eliminate painful, abnormal motion across the instability site.

17. How soon after spine surgery can I return to work, driving, and sports?

Recovery timelines vary based on procedure complexity and individual healing rates. For simple minimally invasive decompression procedures, patients typically resume light desk work and personal driving within 1 to 2 weeks. For spinal fusion or complex reconstructive operations, light activity resumes in 2 to 4 weeks, with a return to full office duties by 4 to 6 weeks. High-impact sports, heavy physical labor, and strenuous exercise are restricted for 3 to 6 months until follow-up imaging confirms solid structural healing and muscular stabilization.
