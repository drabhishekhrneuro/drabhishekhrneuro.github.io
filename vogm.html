Vein of Galen Aneurysmal Malformation (VOGM)

What Is a Vein of Galen Malformation?

Imagine your brain's circulatory system as a delicate network where high-pressure blood from arteries must flow through tiny capillary beds to slow down before gently entering the veins that return blood to the heart.

A Vein of Galen Malformation (VOGM) is a rare congenital blood vessel abnormality where high-pressure arteries connect directly to a major deep vein at the base of the brain—the Great Vein of Galen—without passing through capillaries.

Because there are no capillaries to reduce the force of the blood, high-pressure arterial blood rushes directly into this deep vein, causing it to balloon and stretch into a large, high-flow vessel. In infants and young children, this massive influx of blood forces the heart to work overtime to keep up with the rapid circulation, leading to severe heart strain, fluid accumulation and increased pressure inside the developing head.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms often appear shortly after birth or during early infancy, depending on how much blood is flowing through the malformation:

Neonatal Heart Strain or Failure

Rapid breathing, poor feeding, bluish skin tone or signs of heart strain shortly after birth as the heart struggles to keep up with high-pressure blood flow.

Rapidly Increasing Head Size (Macrocephaly)

A baby’s head growing unusually fast due to fluid buildup or high venous pressure inside the skull.

Prominent Scalp Veins

Clearly visible, swollen or dilated blood vessels beneath the skin of the forehead and scalp.

Developmental Delays & Muscle Stiffness

Delays in reaching age-appropriate motor milestones, accompanied by stiffness or weakness in the arms and legs as high pressure affects surrounding brain tissue.

Hydrocephalus

Increased pressure from excess spinal fluid buildup inside the brain's ventricles, causing irritability, excessive crying or poor feeding.

How is it Discovered?

Early and precise diagnosis is crucial for planning timely, life-saving neurointerventional care:

  • Prenatal & Pediatric Ultrasound: Often detected before birth during routine prenatal ultrasounds using Doppler imaging, which shows fast-flowing blood within the dilated vein.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MRA: Comprehensive, high-resolution imaging that details the structure of the brain tissue, maps out blood flow patterns and checks for signs of hydrocephalus.
  • CT Angiography (CTA): A fast scan that creates detailed 3D maps of the blood vessels, helping evaluate skull bones and high-flow connections.
  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA): The gold-standard diagnostic procedure performed in a cath lab, where micro-catheters map every individual feeding artery supplying the malformation.

How is it Treated?

Treatment requires a specialized, multidisciplinary approach focused on gently closing off the high-pressure connections while supporting heart function:

  • Endovascular Embolization: The primary and most effective treatment, performed by navigating ultra-thin catheters from a blood vessel in the groin up to the brain to place tiny platinum coils or specialized liquid embolic agents that block off the abnormal feeding arteries.
  • Pediatric Intensive & Cardiac Support: Careful stabilization in a specialized neonatal or pediatric intensive care unit (NICU/PICU) using medication to manage heart function and fluid balance prior to procedure planning.
  • Management of Hydrocephalus: If fluid pressure inside the brain remains high, temporary or permanent drainage options (such as an endoscopic third ventriculostomy or shunt) may be used to relieve pressure.
  • Staged Treatment & Long-Term Monitoring: Complex malformations are often treated in planned stages to safely slow down blood flow, followed by regular developmental evaluations to ensure normal brain growth and progress.
