Brain Aneurysm

What Is a Brain Aneurysm?

Imagine one of the blood vessels supplying blood to your brain as a thin-walled garden hose. Under normal conditions, blood flows smoothly through strong, elastic artery walls to nourish your brain tissue.

A brain aneurysm (also called an intracranial or cerebral aneurysm) happens when a weak spot in the wall of a brain artery bulges or balloons outward, filling with blood.

As blood continues to pump through the weak spot, the ballooned area can grow thinner over time—much like an over-inflated balloon. If left untreated, a brain aneurysm carries a risk of leaking or rupturing, causing dangerous bleeding into the surrounding brain space (a subarachnoid hemorrhage) that requires emergency medical attention.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Unruptured brain aneurysms often cause no symptoms unless they grow large enough to press on nearby nerves. A ruptured aneurysm is a medical emergency with sudden, distinct symptoms:

Unruptured – Localized Pressure or Nerve Strain

A persistent ache above or behind one eye, a dilated pupil, vision changes (such as double vision) or numbness on one side of the face.

Ruptured – "Worst Headache of Your Life"

A sudden, excruciatingly severe headache that reaches maximum intensity within seconds (often described as a thunderclap headache).

Nausea and Vomiting

Sudden, intense nausea accompanied by persistent vomiting following the onset of a severe headache.

Neck Stiffness & Light Sensitivity

Severe stiffness in the neck and painful sensitivity to bright lights (photophobia) as blood irritates the brain's lining.

Neurological Changes & Loss of Consciousness

Sudden confusion, drooping eyelid, difficulty speaking, seizures, weakness on one side or brief loss of consciousness.

How is it Discovered?

Early detection and precise imaging are essential to assess rupture risk and plan appropriate treatment:

  • CT Scan (Computed Tomography) & CT Angiography (CTA): A fast, specialized scan that detects blood in or around the brain in cases of sudden headache, combined with contrast dye to map out the shape and size of the blood vessel bulge.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) & MRA: Detailed imaging that uses strong magnetic fields to visualize unruptured aneurysms and examine surrounding brain tissue in high detail.
  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA): The gold-standard diagnostic procedure where a small catheter is guided through a blood vessel to take high-resolution X-ray images of the brain’s blood vessels, showing the exact neck size, structure and blood flow of the aneurysm.

How is it Treated?

Treatment decisions depend on whether the aneurysm is ruptured or unruptured, as well as its size, location, and overall health factors:

  • Endovascular Coiling & Stenting: A minimally invasive procedure where a fine catheter is threaded through the arteries to place soft platinum coils inside the aneurysm (or insert a flow-diverting stent across its opening), blocking blood flow and preventing it from bursting.
  • Surgical Clipping: A traditional neurosurgical procedure where the surgeon accesses the aneurysm through a small opening in the skull and applies a tiny metal clip across the neck of the aneurysm to permanently seal off its blood supply.
  • Monitoring & Medical Management: For small, unruptured aneurysms with low risk of rupture, your neurosurgeon may recommend regular follow-up scans alongside lifestyle modifications, such as strict blood pressure control and smoking cessation.
  • Post-Procedure Care & Rehabilitation: Comprehensive recovery care focused on managing blood vessel spasms (vasospasm), restoring strength and supporting neurological recovery with specialized care.
