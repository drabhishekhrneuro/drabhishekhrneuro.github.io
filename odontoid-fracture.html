Odontoid Fracture

What Is an Odontoid Fracture?

At the very top of your neck, your spine has two specialized vertebrae called C1 (the atlas) and C2 (the axis). Together, they act like a high-tech swivel joint that allows your head to turn smoothly from side to side.

On top of the C2 vertebra sits a distinct, peg-like bone projection that sticks straight upward into C1. This peg is called the odontoid process (or the dens). Think of it like a built-in steering pin: C1 fits over this peg, holding your head in place while letting it rotate left and right.

An odontoid fracture occurs when a strong, sudden force cracks or breaks this bone peg.

Because the odontoid process sits directly in front of the spinal cord at the upper neck, a break here can cause the head and upper spine to become unstable, risking pressure or pinch on the delicate spinal cord behind it.

How Do They Happen?

Odontoid fractures typically occur in two very different ways based on age and activity:

  • High-Impact Trauma in Younger Adults: Caused by high-speed events such as motor vehicle collisions, diving accidents or hard falls in sports, where the neck is violently snapped backward or forward.
  • Low-Impact Falls in Older Adults: In seniors—especially those with osteoporosis (thinning bones)—a simple ground-level trip and fall landing on the chin or forehead can place enough backward bending force on the upper neck to snap the odontoid peg.

Types of Odontoid Fractures

Surgeons categorize odontoid fractures into three main types based on where the bone breaks:

  • Type I: A tiny chip near the very tip of the peg. This type is rare and usually stays very stable.
  • Type II: A break right across the base where the peg joins the main C2 vertebra body. This is the most common type, but because the blood supply at the base is thin, it can sometimes be slow or difficult to heal naturally.
  • Type III: A break that extends deeper down into the sturdy main body of the C2 vertebra. Because there is plenty of healthy bone surface area and blood flow, these usually heal very well.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because the break occurs high up near the base of the skull, symptoms focus heavily on neck stability and pain:

Severe, High-Neck Pain

Sharp, constant pain right at the base of the skull and upper neck immediately following an impact.

Feeling of Instability ("Holding the Head")

Patients often instinctively place their hands on their chin or back of the head to physically hold their head steady, feeling as though it might wobble or slide out of place.

Pain on Turning the Head

Inability or extreme reluctance to turn or rotate the head side to side.

Numbness, Tingling, or Arm Weakness

If the broken bone peg shifts backward and presses against the spinal cord, it can cause "pins and needles," tingling or weakness in the arms, hands or legs.

Emergency Warning: Any sudden trauma followed by severe high-neck pain or tingling in the limbs requires immediate emergency immobilisation (neck collar) and medical evaluation to keep the spine safe.

How Is It Discovered?

Clear imaging is essential to see the exact break line and check if the bone has shifted:

  • Computed Tomography (CT) Scan: The fast, primary diagnostic tool. A CT scan creates sharp, detailed 3D views of the upper neck bones, showing the precise location and alignment of the break.
  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): Used to check whether surrounding ligaments holding the neck are torn, and to ensure the nearby spinal cord is safe and uncompressed.
  • Specialized X-Rays: Plain X-rays—including an "open-mouth" view where you open wide so the camera can look right at the upper neck peg through your teeth—can also spot the fracture.

How Is It Treated?

Treatment depends on the fracture type, how far the bone pieces have moved and the patient's age and overall bone strength:

  • Rigid Neck Collar or Cervical Immobilisation: For stable fractures (like Type I, Type III, or unshifted Type II), wearing a firm neck collar (like a Philadelphia or Aspen collar) for 8 to 12 weeks holds the bone steady while it naturally fuses back together.
  • Anterior Odontoid Screw Fixation: For certain Type II fractures in younger or active patients, a surgeon can insert a single, high-precision micro-screw upward through the front of the neck into the bone peg. This pulls the broken pieces tightly together like a heavy-duty clamp, allowing bone to heal while keeping full neck rotation.
  • Posterior Cervical Fusion (C1–C2 Fusion): If the break is unstable, significantly displaced or unlikely to heal with a collar alone, a surgeon can gently secure C1 and C2 together from the back using small titanium screws and rods. This stabilizes the neck permanently so the spinal cord stays completely safe.
