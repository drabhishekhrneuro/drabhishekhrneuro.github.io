Brain Metastases (Secondary Brain Tumours)

What Are Brain Metastases?

Imagine your body's circulatory and lymphatic systems as a network of highways connecting every organ. While primary brain tumours originate directly within brain tissue, secondary brain tumours—known as brain metastases—begin as primary cancers elsewhere in the body (such as the lungs, breasts, skin, or kidneys).

Brain metastases occur when cancer cells break away from the original tumor site, enter the bloodstream, travel up into the brain, and begin growing as secondary tumors.

Think of the central nervous system as a sensitive, high-security ecosystem:

  • Secondary Colonization: Microscopic cancer cells travel through blood vessels and lodge in small capillary networks within the brain, forming new tumor deposits.
  • Multiple vs. Single Lesions: Brain metastases can occur as a single isolated tumor deposit or as multiple tumors spread across different regions of the brain.
  • Surrounding Swelling (Edema): As the metastasized tumor grows, it triggers localized inflammation and fluid accumulation, creating pressure on surrounding healthy brain tissue.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms often depend on the size, location, and number of metastatic lesions, as well as the amount of surrounding brain swelling:

New or Changing Headaches

Dull, persistent pressure headaches that are often worse in the morning, upon waking, coughing, or bending over.

Focal Neurological Deficits

Progressive weakness, numbness, heaviness, or paralysis on one side of the body, arm, or leg.

Seizures

Unprovoked electrical fits, muscle twitching, or convulsions caused by tumor irritation of the cerebral cortex.

Cognitive & Behavioral Changes

Subtle memory loss, confusion, difficulty concentrating, speech hesitation, or noticeable shifts in mood and personality.

Balance & Coordination Issues

Dizziness, unsteadiness, or difficulty walking smoothly, particularly if tumors develop near balance centers like the cerebellum.

How is it Discovered?

Accurate neuroimaging and systemic cancer staging are essential to identify metastases and map treatment strategies:

  • Contrast-Enhanced Brain MRI: The gold-standard imaging technique. High-contrast MRI identifies even microscopic metastatic lesions and details surrounding brain swelling (edema).
  • CT Angiography & Brain CT: Used in emergency settings to evaluate acute headaches, sudden neurological decline, internal tumor bleeding, or fluid accumulation.
  • Systemic Staging Scans (PET-CT / Chest-Abdomen-Pelvis CT): Comprehensive body imaging performed to locate the original primary tumor site or check for cancer spread in other organs.
  • Surgical Tissue Biopsy or Molecular Testing: Tissue samples analyzed under a microscope—often tested for specific genetic mutations and biomarkers—to guide targeted medical therapies.

How is it Treated?

Treatment is highly personalized based on the number of brain lesions, primary cancer type, overall systemic health, and brain location:

  • Surgical Resection (Craniotomy): Used to surgically remove large, single, or symptomatic metastatic tumors, immediately relieving brain pressure and providing tissue for molecular testing.
  • Stereotactic Radiosurgery (SRS / Gamma Knife / CyberKnife): Advanced, non-invasive precision radiation that delivers highly concentrated beams to destroy single or multiple smaller metastases while sparing surrounding healthy brain tissue.
  • Targeted Therapy & Immunotherapy: Next-generation cancer medications designed to cross the blood-brain barrier and specifically target tumor cell genetic mutations or boost the body's immune response.
  • Whole-Brain Radiation Therapy (WBRT): External radiation delivered across the entire brain when multiple metastases are present to halt widespread microscopic tumor growth.
  • Medical Symptom Management: Specialized medications, such as corticosteroids to rapidly reduce brain swelling and anti-seizure medications to prevent convulsions.
