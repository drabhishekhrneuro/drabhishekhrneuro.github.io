Trigeminal Neuralgia

What Is Trigeminal Neuralgia?

Imagine your facial sensation system as a high-precision electrical grid connected by the trigeminal nerve—the fifth cranial nerve—which branches out across your forehead, cheek, and jaw. This nerve transmits light touch, temperature, and pain signals directly from your face to your brain.

Trigeminal Neuralgia (TN) is a severe, chronic neuropathic pain condition characterized by sudden, excruciating, electric shock-like pain episodes triggered by ordinary daily activities.

Think of the trigeminal nerve as a high-voltage cable with compromised insulation:

  • Vascular Compression (Microvascular Conflict): In most classical cases, an adjacent blood vessel (typically a pulsating artery) presses directly against the trigeminal nerve near where it enters the brainstem. Over time, the constant pulsation wears away the nerve's protective myelin insulation.
  • Short-Circuited Pain Signals: With the insulation worn thin, harmless light touch signals from the face cross over into pain pathways (ephaptic transmission), causing the brain to interpret a light breeze or soft touch as intense, sharp pain.
  • Unpredictable Bursts: Pain arrives in lightning-fast, severe jolts that last from a few seconds to a couple of minutes, often leaving brief pain-free refractory periods between attacks.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Trigeminal neuralgia pain is distinct in its suddenness, extreme intensity, and specific facial distribution:

Electric Shock-Like Facial Pain

Sudden, razor-sharp, stabbing, or burning pain jolts felt along one side of the face, most commonly in the cheek, upper jaw, lower jaw, or teeth.

Mild Trigger Point Sensitivity

Attacks triggered by completely non-painful everyday activities, such as brushing your teeth, shaving, washing your face, applying makeup, eating, drinking, talking, or feeling a gentle breeze on your cheek.

Unilateral Distribution

Pain almost exclusively affects one side of the face at a time, following the V1 (eye/forehead), V2 (cheek/upper jaw), or V3 (lower jaw) branches of the trigeminal nerve.

Paroxysmal Episodes with Remissions

Brief bursts of severe pain lasting seconds to minutes, occurring repeatedly throughout the day. Episodes may occur in clusters for weeks or months followed by spontaneous temporary remission periods.

Secondary Continuous Dull Ache (Atypical TN)

A persistent, background burning or aching sensation that remains between intense stabbing sharp attacks as the condition progresses.

How is it Discovered?

Diagnosis combines detailed clinical nerve history with specialized high-resolution MRI techniques to visualize the nerve-vessel junction:

  • High-Resolution 3T Brain MRI (FIESTA / CISS Sequences): Specialized thin-slice MRI sequences designed to clearly visualize the trigeminal nerve root, pinpoint microscopic vessel compression at the brainstem, and assess nerve distortion.
  • Contrast-Enhanced MRI Brain: Performed to rule out secondary causes of trigeminal neuralgia, such as multiple sclerosis plaques, CPA angle meningiomas, schwannomas, or epidermoid cysts compressing the nerve root.
  • Targeted Neurological Examination: Clinical evaluation of facial sensation and corneal reflexes to differentiate classical trigeminal neuralgia from other facial pain syndromes like persistent idiopathic facial pain or dental disorders.

How is it Treated?

Treatment ranges from specific nerve-stabilizing medications to definitive, high-precision neurosurgical procedures:

  • Targeted Neuropathic Medications: First-line treatment using specific membrane-stabilizing anticonvulsants (such as Carbamazepine or Oxcarbazepine) that calm hyperactive electrical firing in the nerve.
  • Microvascular Decompression (MVD / Jannetta Procedure): The definitive, curative neurosurgical treatment for classical TN. Through a small opening behind the ear (retrosigmoid approach), the surgeon uses an operating microscope to carefully separate the pulsating blood vessel from the trigeminal nerve and insert a tiny Teflon cushion between them, permanently relieving compression without causing facial numbness.
  • Percutaneous Rhizotomy Techniques: Minimally invasive outpatient procedures performed via a needle inserted through the cheek into the Gasserian ganglion—such as Balloon Compression, Radiofrequency Thermocoagulation, or Glycerol Rhizotomy—to selectively deaden pain fibers in patients unsuitable for open surgery.
  • Stereotactic Radiosurgery (Gamma Knife / CyberKnife): A non-invasive precision radiation procedure that delivers a highly focused beam of energy to the trigeminal nerve root, gradually interrupting pain signal transmission over several weeks.
