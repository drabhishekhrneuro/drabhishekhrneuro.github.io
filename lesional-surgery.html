Movement Disorder Surgery & Functional Neurosurgery

Stereotactic Lesional Surgery (Thalamotomy & Pallidotomy)

What Is Stereotactic Lesional Surgery?

Imagine your brain's motor control circuit as a complex network where abnormal electrical feedback loops can cause continuous, disabling physical symptoms. In severe movement disorders like Parkinson's disease, dystonia, or Essential Tremor, specific deep brain structures—such as the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus or the globus pallidus interna (GPi)—become hyperactive. This hyperactivity sends irregular signals down the spinal cord, manifesting as violent tremors, rigidity, or involuntary muscle spasms.

While Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) uses implanted hardware to electrically modulate these circuits, Stereotactic Lesional Surgery takes a direct, hardware-free interventional approach. By creating a tiny, highly localized, millimeter-sized therapeutic thermal ablation (lesion) in the precise faulty node of the circuit, neurosurgeons permanently interrupt the abnormal signal loop, suppressing tremors and motor hyperactivity.

Think of lesional surgery as permanently disconnecting a faulty wire in an overloaded circuit:

  • Hardware-Free Relief: Achieves targeted symptom relief without implanting pulse generators, extension wires, or battery units beneath the skin, eliminating long-term hardware maintenance, charging, or infection risks.
  • Targeted Interventional Modalities: Can be performed via traditional Radiofrequency (RF) Thermal Lesioning (using a specialized micro-electrode) or via incisionless MR-Guided Focused Ultrasound (MRgFUS), which uses focused sound waves guided by real-time MRI without opening the skull.
  • Immediate & Permanent Symptom Suppression: Provides rapid, long-lasting reduction of unilateral hand tremors, painful dystonic contractions, or levodopa-induced dyskinesias.

When Is Lesional Surgery Recommended?

Lesional procedures are indicated for severe, medically refractory movement disorders, particularly when hardware implantation is unsuitable or undesirable:

Severe Medically Refractory Essential Tremor

Disabling hand or arm tremor that severely impairs eating, writing, or dressing and fails to respond to medications (typically treated with VIM Thalamotomy).

Tremor-Dominant Parkinson's Disease

Severe, asymmetric resting tremor or debilitating levodopa-induced dyskinesias (writhing movements) that disrupt daily independence (treated with Thalamotomy or Pallidotomy).

Severe Focal or Segmental Dystonia

Painful, involuntary muscle twisting or abnormal postures (such as cervical dystonia) that fail to improve with oral medications or botulinum toxin injections.

Non-Candidates for Implanted Hardware

Patients who are unable or prefer not to undergo hardware maintenance, those with frequent surgical infection risk factors, or patients unable to comply with regular DBS programming follow-ups.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Sub-millimeter diagnostic imaging and neuro-functional screening dictate target selection:

  • High-Resolution 3T Stereotactic MRI: Fine-cut structural sequences (T1/T2/FGATIR) map deep brain target nuclei with sub-millimeter precision relative to stereotactic reference frames.
  • Multidisciplinary Movement Disorder Evaluation: In-depth assessment by a movement disorder neurologist and functional neurosurgeon to confirm diagnosis, measure baseline tremor severity, and select the target node (VIM, GPi, or STN).
  • Cognitive & Speech Assessment: Screening to evaluate baseline cognitive function, speech clarity, and balance to minimize post-operative side effect risks.
  • Skull Density Ratio (SDR) Assessment (for Focused Ultrasound): CT evaluation of skull bone density to confirm optimal acoustic ultrasound energy transmission through the skull cap.

What Happens During the Surgery?

Lesional surgery is performed under local anesthesia (with the patient awake) to allow real-time functional monitoring and immediate assessment of tremor suppression:

  • Stage 1: Stereotactic Frame & Guidance Setup: A lightweight stereotactic head frame is placed under local anesthesia, followed by guidance imaging to merge real-time coordinates with pre-operative MRI scans.
  • Stage 2: Precision Target Access:
    • Incisionless MRgFUS: The patient rests inside the MRI scanner wearing a specialized ultrasound helmet. No incision or skull drilling is performed.
    • Radiofrequency (RF) Lesioning: A small keyhole entry point (twist-drill hole) is made in the skull under local anesthesia.
  • Stage 3: Functional Mapping & Test Heating:
    • MRgFUS: Low-power ultrasound energy slightly warms the target tissue (sub-lethal temperature) to temporarily suppress tremor while confirming no speech or sensory side effects occur.
    • RF Lesioning: Micro-electrode stimulation tests electrical signals in the target node to confirm immediate tremor arrest before thermal ablation.
  • Stage 4: Therapeutic Thermal Ablation: Once the exact focal point is verified, temperature is raised to achieve permanent, pinpoint thermal coagulation (ablation) of the target cell cluster.
  • Stage 5: Verification & Immediate Assessment: Tremor arrest, motor control, and neurological function are re-tested immediately on the operating table, and guidance scans verify the millimeter size and location of the lesion.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery is rapid, with immediate visual confirmation of tremor suppression and minimal post-procedural downtime:

  • Immediate Functional Improvement: Tremor reduction or dyskinesia relief is typically evident immediately on the operating table or within hours of the procedure.
  • Short Hospital Stay (Same-Day to 24 Hours): Patients are monitored briefly in the ward for balance stability and neurological status before returning home. Incisionless FUS patients often go home the same day.
  • Management of Mild Temporary Symptoms: Transient mild unsteady gait (ataxia), subtle speech slurring, or local scalp soreness may occur post-procedure, typically resolving over several days to weeks.
  • Long-Term Follow-Up & Rehabilitation: Follow-up MRI scans at 1 to 3 months verify lesion resolution, while targeted physical or occupational therapy helps fine-tune hand coordination and functional independence.
