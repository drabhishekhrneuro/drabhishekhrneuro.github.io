Brain Meningioma

What Is a Meningioma?

Imagine your brain and spinal cord enclosed inside a protective, multi-layered wrapping called the meninges. This protective lining acts as a durable inner shield between the soft brain tissue and the hard bony skull.

A meningioma is a primary brain tumour that arises from the specialized cells (meningothelial cells) of this protective outer covering.

Think of the meninges as the brain's protective inner wallpaper:

  • Extra-Axial Growth: Meningiomas originate outside the brain tissue itself (extra-axial). Instead of invading into the brain like some primary brain tumors, they typically push inward against the brain surface as they expand.
  • Predominantly Benign: The vast majority (over 80-85%) of meningiomas are non-cancerous (Grade 1, slow-growing). A smaller percentage can be atypical (Grade 2) or malignant (Grade 3, aggressive).
  • Gradual Compression: Because they usually grow very slowly over many years, the brain often adapts to their presence until the tumour becomes large enough to press on adjacent brain structures or cranial nerves.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Because meningiomas grow slowly, symptoms develop gradually and depend on the tumour's location along the skull base or surface lining:

Persistent, Gradual Headaches

Dull pressure headaches that slowly worsen over time as the expanding tumour increases localized pressure inside the head.

Seizures

Unprovoked fits or muscle twitching caused by the tumour pressing against and irritating the outer cortex of the brain.

Focal Weakness or Numbness

Gradual progressive weakness, clumsiness, or loss of sensation in an arm, leg, or face depending on the brain area compressed.

Vision, Smell, or Hearing Changes

Blurred sight, loss of side vision, smell loss (anosmia), or ringing/hearing loss when tumours grow near the optic nerves, olfactory tract, or skull base.

Memory Loss & Personality Shifts

Subtle cognitive changes, concentration difficulties, or emotional shifts when tumours press against the frontal or temporal lobes.

How is it Discovered?

Detailed neuroimaging accurately identifies the tumour's origin, dural attachments, and relation to critical blood vessels:

  • Contrast-Enhanced Brain MRI: The definitive gold-standard imaging technique. High-contrast MRI clearly visualizes the meningioma, its characteristic "dural tail" sign (attachment to the brain lining), and any surrounding brain swelling.
  • Computed Tomography (CT) Scan: High-resolution CT imaging used to evaluate calcification within the tumour and assess whether the tumour has caused thickening or erosion in the surrounding skull bone (hyperostosis).
  • Digital Subtraction Angiography (DSA) / CTA: Pre-surgical vascular mapping performed for large or skull-base meningiomas to identify feeding arteries and evaluate options for preoperative embolization.
  • Histopathological Grading: Surgical tissue samples are analyzed under a microscope to confirm the exact World Health Organization (WHO) grade (Grade 1, 2, or 3) to guide long-term care.

How is it Treated?

Management depends on tumour size, location, growth rate, WHO grade, and patient symptoms:

  • Active Observation ("Wait and Scan"): Small, asymptomatic, or incidentally discovered Grade 1 meningiomas are often safely monitored with regular neurological exams and annual MRI scans.
  • Microsurgical Resection (Craniotomy / Skull Base Surgery): High-precision neurosurgery aiming for complete removal of the tumour and its dural attachment (Simpson Grade 1 resection) while protecting adjacent brain tissue and nerves.
  • Preoperative Embolization: A neurointerventional procedure performed shortly before surgery to block blood flow to large, vascular tumours, reducing intraoperative bleeding and making surgical removal safer.
  • Stereotactic Radiosurgery (Gamma Knife / CyberKnife): Highly focused precision radiation used for small-to-medium tumours, deep skull-base locations risky for complete surgical removal, or recurring lesions to halt growth while preserving function.
  • Fractionated Radiotherapy: External beam radiation reserved for atypical (Grade 2) or malignant (Grade 3) meningiomas, or following incomplete resection of complex tumors to prevent recurrence.
