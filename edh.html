Extradural Hematoma (Epidural Hematoma)

What Is an Extradural Hematoma?

Your brain is wrapped in three protective covers inside your skull and the outermost layer is a tough, leather-like membrane called the dura.

An extradural hematoma (also called an epidural hematoma) occurs when blood collects in the space between the inside of the skull bone and this tough outer dura layer. Think of it like a localized blister of blood forming between a hard outer helmet and its inner liner.

This usually happens after a hard impact to the head—such as a fall, road traffic accident or sports injury—that causes a fracture in the skull bone. As the bone cracks, it can tear a nearby artery (most commonly the middle meningeal artery). Because blood in arteries is under high pressure, it can pool rapidly, peeling the dura away from the bone and pressing inward against the brain.

The "Lucid Interval" & Key Symptoms

Extradural hematomas are known for a very specific pattern called the lucid interval: a person might get knocked out briefly, wake up feeling completely fine for a short period and then suddenly deteriorate as the blood pocket expands.

A Brief Loss of Consciousness

Passing out immediately after the impact, followed by a period of total clarity.

Rapidly Worsening Headache

A severe, persistent headache that gets progressively worse over minutes or hours.

Nausea & Repeated Vomiting

Caused by the rising pressure inside the skull.

Confusion & Drowsiness

Feeling increasingly sleepy, confused or struggling to stay awake.

Unequal Pupil Sizes

One pupil (the black center of the eye) becomes noticeably larger or dilated compared to the other.

Weakness on One Side

Feeling weakness or numbness in the arm or leg on the opposite side of the head injury.

Emergency Warning: An extradural hematoma is a medical emergency. If someone experiences a severe blow to the head followed by worsening headache, confusion or pupil changes, seek immediate emergency hospital care.

How Is It Discovered?

Because an extradural hematoma expands quickly, rapid diagnosis is critical:

  • Emergency Brain CT Scan: A Computed Tomography (CT) scan is the gold standard diagnostic test. It takes seconds to perform and clearly shows the blood collection as a distinct, lens-shaped (biconvex) mass pressing against the brain.
  • Neurological Examination: Assessing the patient's level of consciousness (using the Glasgow Coma Scale), checking pupil reactions, and evaluating motor strength.

How Is It Treated?

When caught early, extradural hematomas have an excellent recovery rate because the underlying brain tissue itself is often uninjured—it is simply being compressed.

  • Emergency Surgical Evacuation (Craniotomy): If the blood pool is large or causing pressure on the brain, a neurosurgeon performs an emergency craniotomy. A small section of skull bone is temporarily removed, the blood clot is gently cleared out, the bleeding blood vessel is sealed and the bone is securely placed back. Think of this like opening a safety valve to instantly release built-up pressure.
  • Observation & Intensive Care: Small, thin blood collections that cause no symptoms or pressure can sometimes be monitored closely in an intensive care unit (ICU) with repeated CT scans to ensure they resolve on their own.
  • Recovery: Most patients recover remarkably well after timely surgery, returning to their normal daily lives once the pressure is relieved and the incision heals.
