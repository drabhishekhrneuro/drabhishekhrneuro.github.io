Obstructive Hydrocephalus (Non-Communicating Hydrocephalus)

What Is Obstructive Hydrocephalus?

Imagine your brain's internal plumbing system—a connected network of fluid-filled chambers called ventricles. Deep inside the brain, a clear, protective fluid called cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is constantly produced. CSF acts as a essential shock absorber, nourishing brain tissue, removing metabolic waste, and maintaining a healthy balance of pressure inside the skull.

Obstructive hydrocephalus (also known as non-communicating hydrocephalus) occurs when a physical blockage develops along one of the narrow connecting channels between these internal fluid chambers.

Think of your brain's fluid pathways as a flowing river system with vital narrow gateways:

  • Physical Blockage in Fluid Channels: CSF continues to be produced at a steady, normal rate, but cannot pass through narrow doorways—such as the Foramen of Monro, the Aqueduct of Sylvius, or the Fourth Ventricle outlet—due to a physical barrier.
  • Pressure Buildup (Dammed Fluid): Because the fluid has nowhere to go, it accumulates behind the blockage, causing the ventricles upstream to stretch, enlarge, and exert elevated pressure against surrounding brain tissue inside the rigid skull.
  • Causes & Triggers: Common causes include congenital narrowing (Aqueductal Stenosis), colloid cysts, brain tumours (such as cerebellar or pineal lesions), blood clots following a hemorrhage, or inflammatory debris from past infections.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms vary depending on age and how rapidly the blockage develops, ranging from chronic progressive discomfort to acute emergency pressure spikes:

Severe "Pressure-Type" Headaches

Dull or intense headaches that are characteristically worse in the morning, upon waking, or when coughing, bending over, or straining.

Nausea & Projectile Vomiting

Sudden bouts of intense nausea and unexplained vomiting caused by rising pressure inside the skull.

Visual Changes & "Sunsetting" Eyes

Blurred vision, double vision (diplopia), temporary grayouts, or in infants, an inability to look upward with eyes driven downward ("sunsetting" appearance).

Unsteadiness & Gait Instability

Clumsiness, loss of balance, or feeling heavy-legged and magnetic while walking due to pressure on motor pathways surrounding the enlarged ventricles.

Rapid Head Enlargement (In Infants)

In babies whose skull bones have not yet fused, a rapidly growing head circumference, bulging soft spot (fontanelle), and irritability.

Sudden Lethargy & Drowsiness (Medical Emergency)

Progressive sleepiness, confusion, or difficulty waking up, signaling severe, life-threatening intracranial pressure requiring urgent neurosurgical intervention.

How is it Discovered?

Diagnostic imaging accurately pinpoints the exact site of blockage, measures ventricle enlargement, and evaluates CSF dynamics:

  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The primary diagnostic scan. MRI clearly visualizes enlarged ventricles upstream of the blockage, pinpoints the obstructive mass or membrane, and shows fluid seepage (periventricular edema) through ventricle walls.
  • Cine phase-contrast MRI (CSF Flow Study): Specialized dynamic MRI sequences that directly measure real-time CSF velocity and flow patterns through narrow passages like the Aqueduct of Sylvius.
  • Non-Contrast CT Scan: A rapid emergency scan used to quickly confirm acute ventricle enlargement, assess brain swelling, and evaluate immediate intracranial pressure risk.
  • Fundoscopic Eye Examination: An evaluation of the back of the eye by an ophthalmologist to check for swelling of the optic nerve (papilledema), a classic sign of elevated pressure inside the skull.

How is it Treated?

Treatment focuses on bypassing or removing the physical barrier to safely restore normal CSF circulation and relieve pressure on the brain:

  • Endoscopic Third Ventriculostomy (ETV): A modern, minimally invasive procedure where a slender camera (endoscope) is guided into the brain's fluid chambers to create a tiny bypass hole in the floor of the third ventricle, allowing CSF to flow naturally around the blockage without needing a permanent implant.
  • Ventriculoperitoneal (VP) Shunt Placement: Placing a small, flexible silicone tube with a pressure-regulating valve that channels excess CSF from the brain's ventricles under the skin into the abdominal cavity (peritoneum), where it is safely absorbed.
  • Surgical Removal of Obstructive Cause: Directly removing the underlying mass, such as a colloid cyst, posterior fossa tumour, or membrane, to unblock the natural CSF pathway permanently.
  • External Ventricular Drain (EVD): A temporary external drainage catheter inserted in emergency settings to rapidly relieve critical intracranial pressure while preparing for definitive surgical repair.
