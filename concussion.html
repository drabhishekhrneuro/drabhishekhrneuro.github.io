Head Injury: Concussion

What Is a Concussion?

Think of your brain as a soft, delicate organ floating in a protective bath of fluid inside the hard, bony shell of your skull. Under normal circumstances, this fluid acts like a built-in shock absorber whenever you move.

A concussion is a mild type of traumatic brain injury caused by a sudden bump, blow or jolt to the head or body. When this force happens, the brain rapidly slides back and forth, sloshing against the tough inner walls of the skull.

Imagine a passenger wearing a seatbelt inside a stopping car—the sudden stop causes a quick, forceful shift. This rapid movement doesn't usually cause bleeding or structural damage that shows up on routine scans, but it briefly stretches brain cells and triggers a temporary biochemical disturbance. Think of it like a temporary computer system glitch rather than a broken hardware part.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms of a concussion don't always appear immediately; some can take hours or even days to become noticeable. They generally fall into four main categories:

Physical Symptoms

Persistent headache or a feeling of pressure in the head, nausea or vomiting, dizziness, balance issues, sensitivity to light or noise and feeling sluggish or hazy.

Thinking & Memory Changes

Difficulty concentrating, feeling mentally "foggy," taking longer to answer questions or short-term memory gaps (such as forgetting what happened right before or after the bump).

Emotional & Mood Shifts

Unusual irritability, feeling extra emotional, anxiety or noticeable mood swings without an obvious trigger.

Sleep Disturbances

Sleeping much more or less than usual or having trouble falling asleep.

Red Flag Warning Signs: Seek immediate emergency medical care if you observe worsening headaches that won't go away, repeated vomiting, slurred speech, increasing confusion, unequal pupil sizes, weakness on one side of the body, seizures or an inability to wake up.

How Is It Discovered?

Because a concussion is a functional issue rather than a structural tear, standard brain scans are often completely normal:

  • Clinical & Neurological Exam: A thorough evaluation checking memory, concentration, vision, balance, coordination and reflexes.
  • Symptom & Cognitive Checklists: Standardized questionnaires used by medical professionals to rate the severity of symptoms.
  • Brain Imaging (CT or MRI): Scans are generally reserved for cases where doctors suspect a more serious structural injury, such as internal bleeding, a skull fracture or a brain bruise.

How Is It Managed & Treated?

The cornerstone of recovering from a concussion is giving the brain time to rest and reset safely:

  • Initial Relative Rest: Resting both physically and mentally for the first 24 to 48 hours. This means limiting strenuous exercise as well as heavy screen time (phones, video games, TV) that forces the brain to process intense light and fast-moving information. Time to take a vacation.
  • Gradual Return-to-Activity: Slowly reintroducing everyday routines, light reading and short walks as symptoms improve. If symptoms flare up, scale back slightly and rest.
  • Gradual Return-to-Play/School Protocol: Athletes and students should follow a step-by-step clearance protocol under medical supervision before resuming contact sports or full school workloads to prevent secondary injuries.
  • Symptom-Specific Therapy: For persistent symptoms lasting longer than a couple of weeks, targeted physical therapy (for neck or balance issues) or vision therapy can help speed up a complete recovery.
