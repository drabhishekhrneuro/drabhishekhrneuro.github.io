Burr Hole Drainage Procedure

What Is a Burr Hole Drainage Procedure?

Imagine your brain enclosed within the protective vault of the skull. When certain types of fluid collections or liquid blood clots accumulate between the brain and its outer coverings—most commonly in chronic subdural hematomas—they form an encapsulated pool of liquefied fluid that exerts slow, progressive pressure on the brain.

A burr hole drainage procedure is a minimally invasive neurosurgical operation where one or two small, coin-sized holes (burr holes) are made through the skull bone to safely drain and wash out liquid blood collections without needing a large open craniotomy.

Think of this procedure as creating a controlled drainage port for trapped fluid:

  • Minimally Invasive Access: Instead of opening a wide bone flap, the neurosurgeon makes a small incision in the scalp and creates a tiny, thumbnail-sized opening in the skull bone.
  • Fluid Liquefaction & Irrigation: Chronic blood collections typically turn into a dark, motor-oil-like liquid over several weeks. This allows the surgeon to gently irrigate and flush out the fluid cavity through the small burr hole.
  • Gentle Decompression: The primary objective is to safely relieve pressure off the surface of the brain, allowing the brain to slowly expand back against the skull while minimizing surgical trauma.

When Is a Burr Hole Procedure Recommended?

This procedure is primarily indicated for fluid or blood collections that are fluid rather than solid:

Chronic Subdural Hematoma (CSDH)

Slow accumulations of old blood and fluid beneath the dura membrane, frequently occurring weeks after a minor head injury in older adults or individuals taking blood thinners.

Progressive Neurological Symptoms & Headaches

When a fluid collection grows large enough to cause persistent headaches, confusion, lethargy, difficulty walking, or progressive weakness in the arms or legs.

Subdural Hygroma

Collections of clear cerebrospinal fluid trapped in the subdural space following trauma or previous surgery that cause mass effect and pressure.

Certain Brain Abscesses or Cyst Aspirations

Targeted drainage of localized fluid-filled infection pockets or cysts when an open resection is not immediately required.

How Is the Procedure Planned & Prepared?

Preoperative assessment focuses on defining the fluid volume, evaluating patient fitness, and correcting clotting parameters:

  • Non-Contrast Brain CT Scan: Detailed imaging to measure the exact thickness of the subdural collection, assess midline brain shift, and check for any acute solid components.
  • Coagulation & Blood Thinner Reversal: Essential laboratory testing to evaluate platelet counts and clotting function, with prompt reversal or withholding of anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications prior to surgery.
  • Surgical Site Marking: Planning the exact placement of the burr holes (typically one near the frontal region and one near the parietal region over the fluid pocket) using anatomical landmarks or CT guidance.
  • Anesthetic Planning: The procedure can often be performed safely under local anesthesia with light intravenous sedation, or under general anesthesia depending on patient comfort and medical status.

What Happens During the Surgery?

The procedure is executed systematically through precise, minimally invasive steps:

  • Stage 1: Scalp Anesthesia & Incision: Under local or general anesthesia, one or two small linear or curved incisions (about 2 to 3 cm) are made in the scalp over the targeted areas of the fluid collection.
  • Stage 2: Drilling the Burr Hole: Using a specialized surgical perforator drill, a small hole (roughly 10 to 14 mm in diameter) is carefully created through the skull bone while protecting the underlying dura membrane.
  • Stage 3: Dural Opening & Fluid Evacuation: The tough outer membrane (dura) is opened, allowing the dark, liquefied blood or fluid to escape under pressure. Gentle warm saline irrigation is used to thoroughly wash out remaining debris from the subdural space.
  • Stage 4: Subdural Drain Placement: A soft, flexible silicone drainage tube (subdural drain) is threaded gently into the cavity to continue draining residual fluid over the next 24 to 48 hours.
  • Stage 5: Scalp Closure: The small bone openings are left as they are (or covered with bone wax/substitutes if needed), and the scalp incisions are closed with sutures or staples.

Post-Operative Recovery & Rehabilitation

Recovery following burr hole drainage emphasizes gradual brain re-expansion, drain management, and monitoring for recurrence:

  • Bed Rest & Drain Management (First 24–48 Hours): Patients typically rest flat or with a slight head elevation while the soft subdural drain collects remaining fluid. The drain is removed once output tapers off.
  • Post-Operative CT Imaging: A follow-up brain CT scan is performed after drain removal to confirm that the brain has re-expanded nicely and that the fluid collection has significantly decreased.
  • Incision & Suture Care: Scalp incisions heal rapidly, and sutures or staples are generally removed in the clinic around 7 to 10 days post-op.
  • Activity Restrictions & Follow-Up: Strenuous lifting and straining are restricted for a few weeks. Because chronic subdural hematomas have a known tendency to recur, follow-up clinical checks and repeat imaging are scheduled to ensure complete resolution.
