Cerebellar Tumours

What Is a Cerebellar Tumour?

Imagine the brain as a complex computer control center, where the cerebellum is the specialized mini-brain sitting at the back and base of the head, right above the brainstem. Its primary role is to act as the body's master coordinator—regulating balance, posture, fine motor precision, and fluid movement.

A cerebellar tumour is an abnormal growth of cells within this posterior region (posterior fossa) of the brain.

Think of the cerebellum as the body's delicate internal gyroscope:

  • Primary vs. Secondary Tumours: These growths can arise directly within the tissue of the cerebellum (such as pilocytic astrocytomas, medulloblastomas, or hemangioblastomas) or spread from cancers elsewhere in the body (metastatic brain tumours).
  • Confined Space: Because the posterior fossa is a tight, crowded compartment bounded by the hard base of the skull, even small tumours can quickly press on balance centers or crowd neighboring brainstem structures.
  • Fluid Pathway Blockage (Hydrocephalus): The cerebellum sits right next to the fourth ventricle—a key drainage channel for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Tumours here frequently compress this channel, blocking fluid outflow and raising pressure throughout the skull.

Key Symptoms & What to Look For

Symptoms of cerebellar tumours reflect loss of motor coordination, altered fluid drainage, or elevated pressure inside the head:

Ataxia (Unsteady Gait & Balance Loss)

Difficulty walking in a straight line, feeling lightheaded or drunk-like while standing, or staggering due to compromised balance coordination.

Loss of Fine Motor Coordination (Dysmetria)

Clumsiness in the hands, difficulty buttoning shirts, writing, or overshooting when reaching for objects.

Morning Headaches & Vomiting

Dull, pressure-type headaches (worse upon waking or coughing) accompanied by sudden nausea or projectile vomiting caused by fluid buildup (hydrocephalus).

Eye Movement Abnormalities (Nystagmus)

Involuntary, rapid side-to-side or up-and-down eye jittering, double vision, or difficulty focusing on objects.

Slurred or Scanning Speech (Dysarthria)

Slow, hesitant, or altered speech rhythm caused by loss of fine motor muscle control over vocal cords and tongue.

How is it Discovered?

Detailed neuroimaging is essential to map the tumour’s origin, check fluid pathways, and evaluate safe surgical routes:

  • Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI): The absolute gold-standard diagnostic tool. High-resolution MRI with contrast clearly delineates the tumour's exact boundaries, solid vs. cystic components, involvement of the fourth ventricle, and pressure on the brainstem.
  • Computed Tomography (CT) Scan: A fast emergency scan used to quickly detect acute hydrocephalus, brain tissue swelling, calcification within the tumour, or bone involvement at the skull base.
  • Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) & Spinal Imaging: In certain tumour types (such as medulloblastoma), MRI of the entire spine or lumbar puncture CSF analysis may be performed to ensure the tumour has not seeded along spinal fluid pathways.
  • Pathological & Molecular Analysis: Tissue acquired during surgical resection or biopsy undergoes microscopic, genetic, and molecular testing to guide tailored post-surgical treatment.

How is it Treated?

Treatment is multi-faceted and aims to relieve pressure inside the skull, restore fluid flow, and safely remove the tumour mass:

  • Hydrocephalus Management: Placement of a temporary external ventricular drain (EVD) or endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV) to safely divert blocked spinal fluid and lower intracranial pressure before or during main surgery.
  • Microsurgical Resection (Suboccipital Craniotomy): The primary treatment where a neurosurgeon makes an opening at the back of the head, using high-power operating microscopes, intraoperative neuronavigation, and nerve monitoring to carefully excise the tumour while protecting critical brainstem pathways.
  • Stereotactic Radiosurgery (Gamma Knife / CyberKnife): Highly targeted, concentrated radiation delivered to precise points for small, recurrent, or deep residual tumours to stop their growth while sparing healthy tissue.
  • Adjuvant Radiotherapy & Chemotherapy: Post-surgical systemic treatment regimens customized according to the specific tumour histology and molecular profile (particularly important for aggressive or high-grade lesions).
  • Cerebellar Rehabilitation: Specialized physical, occupational, and balance therapy programs designed to help retrain coordination, gait stability, and motor control during recovery.
